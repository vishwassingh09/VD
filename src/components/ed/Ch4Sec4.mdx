### 4.4 Linear Dielectrics

<div class="ed-theory-box">

#### 4.4.1 Susceptibility, Permittivity, and Dielectric Constant

In an isotropic **linear dielectric** (below dielectric breakdown or saturation), the induced macroscopic polarization $\vec{P}$ is directly proportional to the *total* macroscopic electric field $\vec{E}$:

$$
\vec{P} = \epsilon_0 \chi_e \vec{E}
$$

where $\chi_e$ is the dimensionless **electric susceptibility**. (For anisotropic crystals, $\chi_e$ generalizes to the rank-2 susceptibility tensor $\chi_{e,ij}$ via $P_i = \epsilon_0 \sum_j \chi_{e,ij} E_j$.) Substituting this constitutive relation into $\vec{D} = \epsilon_0 \vec{E} + \vec{P}$ yields

$$
\vec{D} = \epsilon_0(1 + \chi_e)\vec{E} = \epsilon \vec{E}
$$

where the **permittivity** $\epsilon$ and dimensionless **relative permittivity (dielectric constant)** $\epsilon_r$ are defined by

$$
\epsilon \equiv \epsilon_0 (1 + \chi_e), \qquad \epsilon_r \equiv \frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon_0} = 1 + \chi_e
$$

Taking the curl gives $\nabla \times \vec{D} = \nabla \times \vec{P} = -\epsilon_0 \vec{E} \times \nabla \chi_e$. Consequently, at interfaces where $\chi_e$ jumps discontinuously, $\nabla \times \vec{D} \neq \vec{0}$ and $\vec{D}$ is not determined solely by the free charge $\rho_f$—unless planar, cylindrical, or spherical symmetry forces $\nabla \times \vec{P} = \vec{0}$, or a **homogeneous** linear dielectric fills all space where $\vec{E} \neq \vec{0}$, in which case $\vec{D} = \epsilon_0 \vec{E}_{\text{vac}}$ and $\vec{E} = \vec{E}_{\text{vac}}/\epsilon_r$.

#### 4.4.2 Boundary Value Problems with Linear Dielectrics

Inside a homogeneous isotropic linear dielectric, the bound volume charge density is strictly proportional to the free charge density:

$$
\rho_b = -\nabla \cdot \vec{P} = -\nabla \cdot \left(\frac{\epsilon_0 \chi_e}{\epsilon}\vec{D}\right) = -\frac{\chi_e}{1 + \chi_e}\rho_f
$$

Wherever $\rho_f = 0$, the total volume charge density vanishes ($\rho_{\text{tot}} = \rho_f + \rho_b = 0$) and the electrostatic potential satisfies Laplace's equation $\nabla^2 V = 0$. Across an interface between two linear media (with normal $\hat{n}$ pointing from "below" to "above"), the boundary conditions formulated solely in terms of $V$ and the free surface charge $\sigma_f$ are

$$
V_{\text{above}} = V_{\text{below}}, \qquad \epsilon_{\text{above}}\frac{\partial V_{\text{above}}}{\partial n} - \epsilon_{\text{below}}\frac{\partial V_{\text{below}}}{\partial n} = -\sigma_f
$$

(equivalently $D_{\text{above}}^\perp - D_{\text{below}}^\perp = \sigma_f$ and $\vec{E}_{\text{above}}^\parallel = \vec{E}_{\text{below}}^\parallel$). All standard Chapter 3 techniques—separation of variables, spherical/cylindrical harmonics, and the method of images—apply directly.

#### 4.4.3 Electrostatic Energy in Linear Dielectrics

When an infinitesimal free charge $\delta \rho_f$ is brought in with the dielectric geometry held fixed, the work done on the free charge is $\delta W = \int (\delta\rho_f) V\,d\tau = \int \vec{E}\cdot \delta\vec{D}\,d\tau$ (after integrating by parts using $\nabla\cdot \delta\vec{D} = \delta\rho_f$ and $\vec{E} = -\nabla V$). For a **linear** dielectric ($\vec{D} = \epsilon\vec{E}$), $\vec{E}\cdot\delta\vec{D} = \frac{1}{2}\delta(\vec{D}\cdot\vec{E})$, so integrating from zero field gives the total stored energy:

$$
W = \frac{1}{2}\int \vec{D}\cdot\vec{E}\,d\tau = \frac{1}{2}\int \epsilon E^2\,d\tau
$$

Unlike $W_{\text{vac}} = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$ (which tracks only the macroscopic electrostatic energy of fixed total charge), $W = \frac{1}{2}\int \vec{D}\cdot\vec{E}\,d\tau$ includes the internal mechanical ("spring") energy stored in polarizing the molecules as external agents assemble the free charges. This formula fails for nonlinear, dissipative, or frozen-in (electret) media.

#### 4.4.4 Forces on Dielectrics

Because bound charge accumulates near opposite free charge, a dielectric is drawn into regions of stronger electric field (e.g., into a capacitor via its nonuniform fringing field). Virtual work bypasses explicit calculation of the fringing fields. If the dielectric shifts by $dx$ at **constant free charge $Q$**, $W = \frac{Q^2}{2C}$ and the electrical force is

$$
F_x = -\left.\frac{\partial W}{\partial x}\right|_Q = \frac{Q^2}{2C^2}\frac{dC}{dx} = \frac{1}{2}V^2\frac{dC}{dx}
$$

If instead the system is held at **constant potential $V$** by a battery, energy balance includes the work $V\,dQ$ supplied by the battery, $dW = -F_x\,dx + V\,dQ$, yielding the identical physical force:

$$
F_x = -\left.\frac{\partial W}{\partial x}\right|_V + V\frac{dQ}{dx} = -\frac{1}{2}V^2\frac{dC}{dx} + V^2\frac{dC}{dx} = +\frac{1}{2}V^2\frac{dC}{dx}
$$

<ProblemCard num="4.18" title="Two Dielectric Slabs in a Parallel-Plate Capacitor" tag="Griffiths 4.18 · Displacement, Bound Charge & Consistency Check">
<div slot="problem">

The space between the plates of a parallel-plate capacitor is filled with two slabs of linear dielectric material. Each slab has thickness $a$, so the total distance between the plates is $2a$. Slab 1 (top) has dielectric constant $\epsilon_{r1} = 2$, and Slab 2 (bottom) has dielectric constant $\epsilon_{r2} = 1.5$. The free charge density on the top plate is $+\sigma$ and on the bottom plate is $-\sigma$.

(a) Find the electric displacement $\vec{D}$ in each slab.

(b) Find the electric field $\vec{E}$ in each slab.

(c) Find the polarization $\vec{P}$ in each slab.

(d) Find the potential difference between the plates.

(e) Find the location and amount of all bound charge.

(f) Now that you know all the charge (free and bound), recalculate the field in each slab, and confirm your answer to (b).

</div>
<div slot="answer">

Let $\hat{n}$ denote the unit vector pointing downward (from the $+\sigma$ plate toward the $-\sigma$ plate).

(a) $\vec{D}_1 = \vec{D}_2 = \sigma\,\hat{n}$.

(b) $\vec{E}_1 = \dfrac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{n}$, $\vec{E}_2 = \dfrac{2\sigma}{3\epsilon_0}\,\hat{n}$.

(c) $\vec{P}_1 = \dfrac{\sigma}{2}\,\hat{n}$, $\vec{P}_2 = \dfrac{\sigma}{3}\,\hat{n}$.

(d) $V = \dfrac{7\sigma a}{6\epsilon_0}$.

(e) $\rho_b = 0$ everywhere; $\sigma_b = -\dfrac{\sigma}{2}$ (top of Slab 1), $+\dfrac{\sigma}{2}$ (bottom of Slab 1), $-\dfrac{\sigma}{3}$ (top of Slab 2), $+\dfrac{\sigma}{3}$ (bottom of Slab 2). Net bound surface charge at the interface is $+\dfrac{\sigma}{6}$.

(f) Summing the infinite-sheet fields of free and bound layers gives $E_1 = \dfrac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}$ and $E_2 = \dfrac{2\sigma}{3\epsilon_0}$ downward, matching (b).

</div>
<div slot="solution">

Let $\hat{n}$ be the unit vector pointing downward from the top plate ($+\sigma$) to the bottom plate ($-\sigma$).

**(a) Electric displacement:** Because the system has planar symmetry, $\nabla \times \vec{P} = \vec{0}$ and $\vec{D}$ is purely vertical. Apply Gauss's law $\oint \vec{D} \cdot d\vec{a} = Q_{f,\text{enc}}$ to a Gaussian pillbox of cross-sectional area $A$ whose top face lies inside the upper conducting plate (where $\vec{D} = \vec{0}$) and whose bottom face lies inside either Slab 1 or Slab 2:

$$
D A = \sigma A \implies \vec{D}_1 = \vec{D}_2 = \sigma\,\hat{n}
$$

**(b) Electric field:** Using $\vec{E} = \vec{D}/\epsilon = \vec{D}/(\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r)$ with $\epsilon_{r1} = 2$ and $\epsilon_{r2} = 3/2$:

$$
\vec{E}_1 = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{n}, \qquad \vec{E}_2 = \frac{\sigma}{(3/2)\epsilon_0}\,\hat{n} = \frac{2\sigma}{3\epsilon_0}\,\hat{n}
$$

**(c) Polarization:** Using $\vec{P} = \epsilon_0 \chi_e \vec{E} = \epsilon_0(\epsilon_r - 1)\vec{E} = \left(1 - \frac{1}{\epsilon_r}\right)\vec{D}$:

$$
\vec{P}_1 = \left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right)\sigma\,\hat{n} = \frac{\sigma}{2}\,\hat{n}, \qquad \vec{P}_2 = \left(1 - \frac{2}{3}\right)\sigma\,\hat{n} = \frac{\sigma}{3}\,\hat{n}
$$

**(d) Potential difference:** Integrating $\vec{E}$ from the bottom plate to the top plate:

$$
V = E_1 a + E_2 a = \left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{3}\right)\frac{\sigma a}{\epsilon_0} = \frac{7\sigma a}{6\epsilon_0}
$$

**(e) Bound charges:** Since $\vec{P}$ is uniform within each slab, the volume bound charge density vanishes: $\rho_b = -\nabla \cdot \vec{P} = 0$. The surface bound charge density on each face is $\sigma_b = \vec{P} \cdot \hat{n}_{\text{out}}$, where $\hat{n}_{\text{out}}$ is the outward normal from that slab ($-\hat{n}$ on top faces, $+\hat{n}$ on bottom faces):

$$
\sigma_{b,1,\text{top}} = -\frac{\sigma}{2}, \qquad \sigma_{b,1,\text{bot}} = +\frac{\sigma}{2}, \qquad \sigma_{b,2,\text{top}} = -\frac{\sigma}{3}, \qquad \sigma_{b,2,\text{bot}} = +\frac{\sigma}{3}
$$

(At the interface between Slabs 1 and 2, the combined bound charge density is $+\sigma/2 - \sigma/3 = +\sigma/6$.)

**(f) Direct field recalculation:** An infinite sheet of surface charge $\sigma_{\text{sheet}}$ produces an electric field $\sigma_{\text{sheet}}/(2\epsilon_0)$ pointing away from the sheet.

- **In Slab 1:** Total surface charge above Slab 1 is $\sigma - \frac{\sigma}{2} = +\frac{\sigma}{2}$; total surface charge below Slab 1 is $\left(+\frac{\sigma}{2} - \frac{\sigma}{3}\right) + \left(+\frac{\sigma}{3} - \sigma\right) = -\frac{\sigma}{2}$. Therefore,

$$
\vec{E}_1 = \frac{+\sigma/2}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{n} - \frac{-\sigma/2}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{n} = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{n}
$$

- **In Slab 2:** Total surface charge above Slab 2 is $\sigma - \frac{\sigma}{2} + \frac{\sigma}{2} - \frac{\sigma}{3} = +\frac{2\sigma}{3}$; total surface charge below Slab 2 is $+\frac{\sigma}{3} - \sigma = -\frac{2\sigma}{3}$. Therefore,

$$
\vec{E}_2 = \frac{+2\sigma/3}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{n} - \frac{-2\sigma/3}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{n} = \frac{2\sigma}{3\epsilon_0}\,\hat{n}
$$

Both results match part (b) exactly.

<ProblemCard num="4.19" title="Half-Filled Parallel-Plate Capacitor (Series vs. Parallel)" tag="Griffiths 4.19 · Capacitance & Boundary Fields">
<div slot="problem">

Suppose you have enough linear dielectric material, of dielectric constant $\epsilon_r$, to half-fill a parallel-plate capacitor of plate area $A$ and separation $d$.

(a) By what fraction is the capacitance increased when the dielectric fills the lower half of the gap across the full area $A$ (configuration a)?

(b) By what fraction is the capacitance increased when the dielectric fills the left half of the area $A/2$ across the full gap $d$ (configuration b)?

For a given potential difference $V$ between the plates, find $\vec{E}$, $\vec{D}$, and $\vec{P}$ in each region, and the free and bound charge densities on all surfaces, for both cases.

</div>
<div slot="answer">

With vacuum capacitance $C_0 = \dfrac{\epsilon_0 A}{d}$ and $\hat{x}$ pointing downward from the higher-potential top plate to the lower-potential bottom plate:

(a) $C_a = \dfrac{2\epsilon_r}{1+\epsilon_r} C_0$, so the fractional increase is $\dfrac{C_a - C_0}{C_0} = \dfrac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 1}$.

- Air: $\vec{E} = \dfrac{2\epsilon_r V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}\hat{x}$, $\vec{D} = \dfrac{2\epsilon_r \epsilon_0 V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}\hat{x}$, $\vec{P} = \vec{0}$.
- Dielectric: $\vec{E} = \dfrac{2V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}\hat{x}$, $\vec{D} = \dfrac{2\epsilon_r \epsilon_0 V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}\hat{x}$, $\vec{P} = \dfrac{2(\epsilon_r - 1)\epsilon_0 V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}\hat{x}$.
- Free charge: $\sigma_f = \pm\dfrac{2\epsilon_r \epsilon_0 V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}$ (top/bottom plates). Bound charge on dielectric: $\sigma_b = \mp\dfrac{2(\epsilon_r - 1)\epsilon_0 V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}$ (top/bottom faces).

(b) $C_b = \dfrac{1+\epsilon_r}{2} C_0$, so the fractional increase is $\dfrac{C_b - C_0}{C_0} = \dfrac{\epsilon_r - 1}{2}$.

- Air: $\vec{E} = \dfrac{V}{d}\hat{x}$, $\vec{D} = \dfrac{\epsilon_0 V}{d}\hat{x}$, $\vec{P} = \vec{0}$.
- Dielectric: $\vec{E} = \dfrac{V}{d}\hat{x}$, $\vec{D} = \dfrac{\epsilon_r \epsilon_0 V}{d}\hat{x}$, $\vec{P} = \dfrac{(\epsilon_r - 1)\epsilon_0 V}{d}\hat{x}$.
- Free charge on top plate: $\sigma_{f,\text{left}} = \dfrac{\epsilon_r \epsilon_0 V}{d}$, $\sigma_{f,\text{right}} = \dfrac{\epsilon_0 V}{d}$ (opposite signs on bottom plate). Bound charge on dielectric: $\sigma_b = \mp\dfrac{(\epsilon_r - 1)\epsilon_0 V}{d}$ (top/bottom faces).

</div>
<div slot="solution">

Let the top plate be at potential $V$, the bottom plate be grounded ($0$), and the unit vector $\hat{x}$ point downward from top to bottom. Without any dielectric, $C_0 = \epsilon_0 A / d$.

**(a) Configuration (a) — Series arrangement (air thickness $d/2$, dielectric thickness $d/2$):**

Let the top and bottom plates carry uniform free charge densities $+\sigma_f$ and $-\sigma_f$, with total charge $Q = \sigma_f A$. By planar symmetry, $\vec{D} = \sigma_f \hat{x}$ in both regions. Thus

$$
E_{\text{air}} = \frac{\sigma_f}{\epsilon_0}, \qquad E_{\text{diel}} = \frac{\sigma_f}{\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r}
$$

The potential difference is

$$
V = E_{\text{air}}\frac{d}{2} + E_{\text{diel}}\frac{d}{2} = \frac{\sigma_f d}{2\epsilon_0}\left(1 + \frac{1}{\epsilon_r}\right) = \frac{Q d}{2\epsilon_0 A}\frac{\epsilon_r + 1}{\epsilon_r}
$$

Hence the capacitance and fractional increase are

$$
C_a = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d}\left(\frac{2\epsilon_r}{1+\epsilon_r}\right) = \frac{2\epsilon_r}{1+\epsilon_r} C_0, \qquad \frac{C_a - C_0}{C_0} = \frac{2\epsilon_r}{1+\epsilon_r} - 1 = \frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 1}
$$

Solving for $\sigma_f$ in terms of $V$ gives $\sigma_f = \frac{2\epsilon_r \epsilon_0 V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}$ on the top plate ($-\sigma_f$ on the bottom plate). Therefore:

- **In air:**

$$
\vec{D}_{\text{air}} = \frac{2\epsilon_r \epsilon_0 V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}\hat{x}, \qquad \vec{E}_{\text{air}} = \frac{2\epsilon_r V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}\hat{x}, \qquad \vec{P}_{\text{air}} = \vec{0}
$$

- **In dielectric:**

$$
\vec{D}_{\text{diel}} = \frac{2\epsilon_r \epsilon_0 V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}\hat{x}, \qquad \vec{E}_{\text{diel}} = \frac{2V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}\hat{x}, \qquad \vec{P}_{\text{diel}} = \epsilon_0(\epsilon_r - 1)\vec{E}_{\text{diel}} = \frac{2(\epsilon_r - 1)\epsilon_0 V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}\hat{x}
$$

Since $\vec{P}_{\text{diel}}$ is uniform, $\rho_b = 0$. On the top face of the dielectric ($\hat{n}_{\text{out}} = -\hat{x}$) and bottom face ($\hat{n}_{\text{out}} = +\hat{x}$), the bound surface charges are

$$
\sigma_{b,\text{top}} = -\frac{2(\epsilon_r - 1)\epsilon_0 V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}, \qquad \sigma_{b,\text{bot}} = +\frac{2(\epsilon_r - 1)\epsilon_0 V}{(\epsilon_r + 1)d}
$$

**(b) Configuration (b) — Parallel arrangement (left area $A/2$ dielectric, right area $A/2$ air):**

Neglecting edge fringing at the central boundary, the electric field is uniform and identical in both halves because each spans the same potential difference $V$ over distance $d$:

$$
\vec{E}_{\text{air}} = \vec{E}_{\text{diel}} = \frac{V}{d}\hat{x}
$$

Using $\vec{D} = \epsilon \vec{E}$ and $\vec{P} = \epsilon_0(\epsilon_r - 1)\vec{E}$:

- **In air (right half):**

$$
\vec{D}_{\text{air}} = \frac{\epsilon_0 V}{d}\hat{x}, \qquad \vec{P}_{\text{air}} = \vec{0}
$$

- **In dielectric (left half):**

$$
\vec{D}_{\text{diel}} = \frac{\epsilon_r \epsilon_0 V}{d}\hat{x}, \qquad \vec{P}_{\text{diel}} = \frac{(\epsilon_r - 1)\epsilon_0 V}{d}\hat{x}
$$

The free charge densities on the top plate are determined by $D^\perp$:

$$
\sigma_{f,\text{left}} = \frac{\epsilon_r \epsilon_0 V}{d}, \qquad \sigma_{f,\text{right}} = \frac{\epsilon_0 V}{d}
$$

with opposite signs on the bottom plate. The bound charge densities on the dielectric are $\rho_b = 0$ inside, and on its top/bottom faces:

$$
\sigma_{b,\text{top}} = -\frac{(\epsilon_r - 1)\epsilon_0 V}{d}, \qquad \sigma_{b,\text{bot}} = +\frac{(\epsilon_r - 1)\epsilon_0 V}{d}
$$

(There is zero bound charge on the vertical interface since $\vec{P}_{\text{diel}}$ is parallel to it.) The total free charge on the top plate is

$$
Q = \frac{A}{2}\left(\sigma_{f,\text{left}} + \sigma_{f,\text{right}}\right) = \frac{\epsilon_0 A V}{2d}(1 + \epsilon_r)
$$

so the capacitance and fractional increase are

$$
C_b = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_r}{2}\right) = \frac{1+\epsilon_r}{2} C_0, \qquad \frac{C_b - C_0}{C_0} = \frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{2}
$$

(Note that $C_b - C_a = C_0\frac{(\epsilon_r - 1)^2}{2(\epsilon_r + 1)} > 0$ for $\epsilon_r > 1$, so the parallel configuration always achieves a larger capacitance.)

<ProblemCard num="4.20" title="Potential of a Uniformly Charged Linear Dielectric Sphere" tag="Griffiths 4.20 · Spherical Symmetry & Gauss's Law for D">
<div slot="problem">

A sphere of linear dielectric material has embedded in it a uniform free charge density $\rho$. Find the potential at the center of the sphere (relative to infinity), if its radius is $R$ and the dielectric constant is $\epsilon_r$.

</div>
<div slot="answer">

$$
V(0) = \frac{\rho R^2}{3\epsilon_0}\left(1 + \frac{1}{2\epsilon_r}\right)
$$

</div>
<div slot="solution">

Because the free charge distribution and dielectric medium are spherically symmetric, we can determine $\vec{D}(r)$ directly from Gauss's law, $\oint \vec{D} \cdot d\vec{a} = Q_{f,\text{enc}}$, using concentric spherical Gaussian surfaces of radius $r$:

- **Inside the sphere ($r < R$):**

$$
D(4\pi r^2) = \rho\left(\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\right) \implies \vec{D} = \frac{\rho r}{3}\hat{r} \implies \vec{E} = \frac{\vec{D}}{\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r} = \frac{\rho r}{3\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r}\hat{r}
$$

- **Outside the sphere ($r > R$):**

$$
D(4\pi r^2) = \rho\left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\right) \implies \vec{D} = \frac{\rho R^3}{3r^2}\hat{r} \implies \vec{E} = \frac{\vec{D}}{\epsilon_0} = \frac{\rho R^3}{3\epsilon_0 r^2}\hat{r}
$$

Integrating $\vec{E}$ from infinity to the center gives the potential $V(0)$:

$$
V(0) = -\int_\infty^0 \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l} = \int_0^R \frac{\rho r}{3\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r}\,dr + \int_R^\infty \frac{\rho R^3}{3\epsilon_0 r^2}\,dr
$$

$$
V(0) = \frac{\rho R^2}{6\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r} + \frac{\rho R^2}{3\epsilon_0} = \frac{\rho R^2}{3\epsilon_0}\left(1 + \frac{1}{2\epsilon_r}\right)
$$

<ProblemCard num="4.21" title="Coaxial Cable Partially Filled with Dielectric" tag="Griffiths 4.21 · Cylindrical Symmetry & Capacitance per Unit Length">
<div slot="problem">

A certain coaxial cable consists of a copper wire of radius $a$, surrounded by a concentric copper tube of inner radius $c$. The space between is partially filled (from radius $b$ out to $c$, with $a < b < c$) with linear dielectric material of dielectric constant $\epsilon_r$. Find the capacitance per unit length of this cable.

</div>
<div slot="answer">

$$
\frac{C}{\ell} = \frac{2\pi\epsilon_0}{\ln(b/a) + \dfrac{1}{\epsilon_r}\ln(c/b)}
$$

</div>
<div slot="solution">

Place a free charge $Q$ on a length $\ell$ of the inner copper wire (so the linear free charge density is $\lambda = Q/\ell$). By cylindrical symmetry, applying Gauss's law $\oint \vec{D}\cdot d\vec{a} = Q_{f,\text{enc}}$ to a coaxial cylinder of radius $s$ ($a < s < c$) and length $\ell$ yields

$$
D(2\pi s \ell) = Q \implies \vec{D} = \frac{Q}{2\pi s \ell}\hat{s}
$$

The electric field in the vacuum gap and the dielectric jacket is therefore

$$
\vec{E} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{Q}{2\pi\epsilon_0 s \ell}\hat{s}, & a < s < b \\ \dfrac{Q}{2\pi\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r s \ell}\hat{s}, & b < s < c \end{cases}
$$

The potential difference between the inner wire and outer tube is

$$
V = -\int_c^a \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l} = \int_a^b \frac{Q}{2\pi\epsilon_0 s \ell}\,ds + \int_b^c \frac{Q}{2\pi\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r s \ell}\,ds = \frac{Q}{2\pi\epsilon_0 \ell}\left[\ln\left(\frac{b}{a}\right) + \frac{1}{\epsilon_r}\ln\left(\frac{c}{b}\right)\right]
$$

Hence the capacitance per unit length is

$$
\frac{C}{\ell} = \frac{Q}{V\ell} = \frac{2\pi\epsilon_0}{\ln(b/a) + \dfrac{1}{\epsilon_r}\ln(c/b)}
$$

</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="4.22" title="Dielectric Cylinder in a Uniform Electric Field" tag="Griffiths 4.22 · 2D Laplace Equation & Cylindrical Harmonics">
<div slot="problem">

A very long cylinder of linear dielectric material is placed in an otherwise uniform electric field $\vec{E}_0$. Find the resulting field within the cylinder. (The radius is $a$, the susceptibility is $\chi_e$, and the axis is perpendicular to $\vec{E}_0$.)

</div>
<div slot="answer">

$$
\vec{E}_{\text{in}} = \frac{1}{1 + \chi_e/2}\vec{E}_0 = \frac{2}{\epsilon_r + 1}\vec{E}_0
$$

</div>
<div slot="solution">

Align the cylinder axis along $\hat{z}$ and take the external field along $\hat{x}$, so $\vec{E}_0 = E_0 \hat{x}$. Because there is no free charge inside or on the cylinder ($\rho_f = 0$, $\sigma_f = 0$), the potential satisfies the 2D Laplace equation $\nabla^2 V(s,\phi) = 0$ both inside ($s < a$) and outside ($s > a$), subject to the boundary conditions:

1. $V_{\text{in}}(a,\phi) = V_{\text{out}}(a,\phi)$,
2. $\epsilon \left.\dfrac{\partial V_{\text{in}}}{\partial s}\right|_{s=a} = \epsilon_0 \left.\dfrac{\partial V_{\text{out}}}{\partial s}\right|_{s=a}$ (equivalently $\epsilon_r \left.\dfrac{\partial V_{\text{in}}}{\partial s}\right|_{s=a} = \left.\dfrac{\partial V_{\text{out}}}{\partial s}\right|_{s=a}$),
3. $V_{\text{out}}(s,\phi) \to -E_0 s \cos\phi$ for $s \gg a$.

Setting the reference potential $V = 0$ on the $yz$-plane ($\phi = \pm \pi/2$), and discarding $\ln s$ terms (which diverge at the origin inside and correspond to nonzero net free charge outside), the general cylindrical harmonic expansions are

$$
V_{\text{in}}(s,\phi) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty s^k \left(a_k \cos k\phi + b_k \sin k\phi\right)
$$

$$
V_{\text{out}}(s,\phi) = -E_0 s \cos\phi + \sum_{k=1}^\infty s^{-k} \left(c_k \cos k\phi + d_k \sin k\phi\right)
$$

Applying boundary condition (1) at $s = a$:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^\infty a^k (a_k \cos k\phi + b_k \sin k\phi) = -E_0 a \cos\phi + \sum_{k=1}^\infty a^{-k} (c_k \cos k\phi + d_k \sin k\phi)
$$

Applying boundary condition (2) at $s = a$:

$$
\epsilon_r \sum_{k=1}^\infty k a^{k-1} (a_k \cos k\phi + b_k \sin k\phi) = -E_0 \cos\phi - \sum_{k=1}^\infty k a^{-k-1} (c_k \cos k\phi + d_k \sin k\phi)
$$

By the linear independence (orthogonality) of $\cos k\phi$ and $\sin k\phi$ on $[0,2\pi]$, all coefficients vanish except $a_1$ and $c_1$. For $k = 1$:

$$
a a_1 = -E_0 a + a^{-1} c_1 \implies c_1 = a^2(a_1 + E_0)
$$

$$
\epsilon_r a_1 = -E_0 - a^{-2} c_1 \implies c_1 = -a^2(\epsilon_r a_1 + E_0)
$$

Equating these two expressions for $c_1$ yields

$$
a_1 + E_0 = -\epsilon_r a_1 - E_0 \implies (\epsilon_r + 1)a_1 = -2E_0 \implies a_1 = -\frac{2}{\epsilon_r + 1}E_0 = -\frac{E_0}{1 + \chi_e/2}
$$

Thus the interior potential is linear in $x = s\cos\phi$:

$$
V_{\text{in}}(s,\phi) = -\frac{E_0}{1 + \chi_e/2} s\cos\phi = -\frac{E_0}{1 + \chi_e/2} x
$$

and the electric field inside the cylinder is uniform:

$$
\vec{E}_{\text{in}} = -\nabla V_{\text{in}} = \frac{1}{1 + \chi_e/2}\vec{E}_0 = \frac{2}{\epsilon_r + 1}\vec{E}_0
$$

</div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="4.23" title="Dielectric Sphere in a Uniform Field via Successive Approximations" tag="Griffiths 4.23 · Iterative Polarization Series">
<div slot="problem">

Find the field inside a sphere of linear dielectric material in an otherwise uniform electric field $\vec{E}_0$ (Example 4.7) by the method of successive approximations: First pretend the field inside is just $\vec{E}_0$, and use $\vec{P} = \epsilon_0 \chi_e \vec{E}$ to write down the resulting polarization $\vec{P}_0$. This polarization generates a field of its own, $\vec{E}_1$, which in turn modifies the polarization by an amount $\vec{P}_1$, which further changes the field by an amount $\vec{E}_2$, and so on. The resulting field is $\vec{E}_0 + \vec{E}_1 + \vec{E}_2 + \cdots$. Sum the series, and compare your answer with Eq. 4.49.

</div>
<div slot="answer">

$$
\vec{E} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \left(-\frac{\chi_e}{3}\right)^n \vec{E}_0 = \frac{1}{1 + \chi_e/3}\vec{E}_0 = \frac{3}{\epsilon_r + 2}\vec{E}_0
$$

which matches Eq. 4.49.

</div>
<div slot="solution">

From Example 4.2, a sphere carrying a uniform polarization $\vec{P}$ produces an exactly uniform electric field in its interior given by

$$
\vec{E}_{\text{self}} = -\frac{1}{3\epsilon_0}\vec{P}
$$

Starting with the zeroth-order field $\vec{E}_0$ inside the sphere, the induced polarization is

$$
\vec{P}_0 = \epsilon_0 \chi_e \vec{E}_0
$$

This uniform polarization generates a first-order depolarization field:

$$
\vec{E}_1 = -\frac{1}{3\epsilon_0}\vec{P}_0 = -\frac{\chi_e}{3}\vec{E}_0
$$

The field $\vec{E}_1$ induces an additional uniform polarization $\vec{P}_1 = \epsilon_0 \chi_e \vec{E}_1 = -\epsilon_0\frac{\chi_e^2}{3}\vec{E}_0$, which in turn generates

$$
\vec{E}_2 = -\frac{1}{3\epsilon_0}\vec{P}_1 = \left(-\frac{\chi_e}{3}\right)^2 \vec{E}_0
$$

By induction, the $n$-th correction to the interior electric field is $\vec{E}_n = \left(-\frac{\chi_e}{3}\right)^n \vec{E}_0$. Summing the geometric series (convergent for $\chi_e < 3$, and analytically valid for all $\chi_e \ge 0$):

$$
\vec{E} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \vec{E}_n = \left[\sum_{n=0}^\infty \left(-\frac{\chi_e}{3}\right)^n\right]\vec{E}_0 = \frac{1}{1 + \chi_e/3}\vec{E}_0
$$

Since $\epsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e$, we have $1 + \chi_e/3 = (\epsilon_r + 2)/3$, giving

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{3}{\epsilon_r + 2}\vec{E}_0
$$

in exact agreement with Eq. 4.49.

</div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="4.24" title="Coated Conducting Sphere in a Uniform Electric Field" tag="Griffiths 4.24 · Multi-Region Spherical Harmonics">
<div slot="problem">

An uncharged conducting sphere of radius $a$ is coated with a thick insulating shell (dielectric constant $\epsilon_r$) out to radius $b$. This object is now placed in an otherwise uniform electric field $\vec{E}_0$. Find the electric field in the insulator.

</div>
<div slot="answer">

With $\vec{E}_0 = E_0 \hat{z}$ and $a < r < b$:

$$
\vec{E}(r,\theta) = \frac{3E_0}{2\left[1 - (a/b)^3\right] + \epsilon_r\left[1 + 2(a/b)^3\right]}\left[\left(1 + \frac{2a^3}{r^3}\right)\cos\theta\,\hat{r} - \left(1 - \frac{a^3}{r^3}\right)\sin\theta\,\hat{\theta}\right]
$$

</div>
<div slot="solution">

Choose the $z$-axis along $\vec{E}_0 = E_0 \hat{z}$ and set the potential of the conducting sphere ($r \le a$) to $V_{\text{in}} = 0$. Since there is no free charge in the insulator ($a < r < b$) or exterior ($r > b$), the potential satisfies $\nabla^2 V = 0$ in both regions, with azimuthal symmetry:

$$
V_{\text{med}}(r,\theta) = \sum_{l=0}^\infty \left(A_l r^l + \frac{\bar{B}_l}{r^{l+1}}\right)P_l(\cos\theta) \qquad (a < r < b)
$$

$$
V_{\text{out}}(r,\theta) = -E_0 r \cos\theta + \sum_{l=0}^\infty \frac{B_l}{r^{l+1}} P_l(\cos\theta) \qquad (r > b)
$$

(Note that $B_0 = 0$ since the total free charge on the conductor is zero.) The boundary conditions are:

1. $V_{\text{med}}(a,\theta) = 0$,
2. $V_{\text{med}}(b,\theta) = V_{\text{out}}(b,\theta)$,
3. $\epsilon_r \left.\dfrac{\partial V_{\text{med}}}{\partial r}\right|_{r=b} = \left.\dfrac{\partial V_{\text{out}}}{\partial r}\right|_{r=b}$.

From condition (1) and orthogonality of $P_l(\cos\theta)$:

$$
A_l a^l + \frac{\bar{B}_l}{a^{l+1}} = 0 \implies \bar{B}_l = -a^{2l+1} A_l \quad \text{for all } l \ge 0
$$

Substituting $\bar{B}_l = -a^{2l+1} A_l$ into conditions (2) and (3) at $r = b$:

- For $l = 0$: since $B_0 = 0$, condition (3) gives $-\epsilon_r \bar{B}_0 / b^2 = 0 \implies \bar{B}_0 = A_0 = 0$.
- For $l \ge 2$: condition (2) gives $B_l = A_l(b^{2l+1} - a^{2l+1})$, while condition (3) gives

$$
\epsilon_r \left[l A_l b^{l-1} + (l+1)\frac{a^{2l+1} A_l}{b^{l+2}}\right] = -(l+1)\frac{B_l}{b^{l+2}} \implies B_l = -\frac{\epsilon_r}{l+1}A_l\left[l b^{2l+1} + (l+1)a^{2l+1}\right]
$$

Since $b > a$ and $\epsilon_r > 0$, $b^{2l+1} - a^{2l+1} > 0$ whereas $-\frac{\epsilon_r}{l+1}[lb^{2l+1} + (l+1)a^{2l+1}] < 0$, forcing $A_l = \bar{B}_l = B_l = 0$ for all $l \neq 1$.

- For $l = 1$ (remembering $P_1(\cos\theta) = \cos\theta$):
  - Condition (2): $-E_0 b + \dfrac{B_1}{b^2} = A_1 b - \dfrac{a^3 A_1}{b^2} \implies B_1 - E_0 b^3 = A_1(b^3 - a^3)$.
  - Condition (3): $\epsilon_r\left(A_1 + \dfrac{2a^3 A_1}{b^3}\right) = -E_0 - \dfrac{2B_1}{b^3} \implies -2B_1 - E_0 b^3 = \epsilon_r A_1(b^3 + 2a^3)$.

Eliminating $B_1$ by adding twice the first equation to the second:

$$
-3 E_0 b^3 = A_1\left[2(b^3 - a^3) + \epsilon_r(b^3 + 2a^3)\right]
$$

$$
A_1 = -\frac{3E_0}{2\left[1 - (a/b)^3\right] + \epsilon_r\left[1 + 2(a/b)^3\right]}
$$

Thus, inside the dielectric shell ($a < r < b$):

$$
V_{\text{med}}(r,\theta) = A_1\left(r - \frac{a^3}{r^2}\right)\cos\theta = -\frac{3E_0}{2\left[1 - (a/b)^3\right] + \epsilon_r\left[1 + 2(a/b)^3\right]}\left(r - \frac{a^3}{r^2}\right)\cos\theta
$$

Taking the negative gradient $\vec{E} = -\frac{\partial V_{\text{med}}}{\partial r}\hat{r} - \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial V_{\text{med}}}{\partial \theta}\hat{\theta}$ yields

$$
\vec{E}(r,\theta) = \frac{3E_0}{2\left[1 - (a/b)^3\right] + \epsilon_r\left[1 + 2(a/b)^3\right]}\left[\left(1 + \frac{2a^3}{r^3}\right)\cos\theta\,\hat{r} - \left(1 - \frac{a^3}{r^3}\right)\sin\theta\,\hat{\theta}\right]
$$

(As $a \to 0$, this reduces to $\frac{3}{\epsilon_r+2}\vec{E}_0$; as $\epsilon_r \to 1$, it recovers the field outside an uncharged conducting sphere in $\vec{E}_0$.)

</div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="4.25" title="Point Charge Above an Interface Between Two Linear Dielectrics" tag="Griffiths 4.25 · Method of Images & Bound Surface Charge">
<div slot="problem">

Suppose the region above the $xy$-plane ($z > 0$) in Example 4.8 is also filled with linear dielectric, of susceptibility $\chi_e'$ (while the region $z < 0$ has susceptibility $\chi_e$). A free point charge $q$ is situated at $(0, 0, d)$ ($d > 0$). Find the potential everywhere.

</div>
<div slot="answer">

With $\epsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e$ and $\epsilon_r' = 1 + \chi_e'$:

$$
V(x,y,z) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r'}\left[\dfrac{q}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z - d)^2}} + \left(\dfrac{\epsilon_r' - \epsilon_r}{\epsilon_r' + \epsilon_r}\right)\dfrac{q}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z + d)^2}}\right], & z > 0 \\[12pt] \dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{2q}{\epsilon_r' + \epsilon_r}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z - d)^2}}, & z < 0 \end{cases}
$$

</div>
<div slot="solution">

We identify all four charge contributions: (i) the free point charge $q$ at $(0,0,d)$, (ii) the polarization bound charge $q_p$ clustering immediately around $q$ in the upper dielectric, (iii) the bound surface charge $\sigma_b$ on the top face ($z = 0^-$) of the lower dielectric, and (iv) the bound surface charge $\sigma_b'$ on the bottom face ($z = 0^+$) of the upper dielectric.

By Eq. 4.39, $q_p = -\frac{\chi_e'}{1+\chi_e'}q$, so the net effective point charge at $(0,0,d)$ is

$$
q_t = q + q_p = \frac{q}{1 + \chi_e'} = \frac{q}{\epsilon_r'}
$$

Let $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ be the cylindrical radius on the $z = 0$ plane. The $z$-component of the electric field at $z = 0$ due to $q_t$ is $-\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{(q/\epsilon_r')d}{(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}}$, while the planar bound sheet $\sigma_t = \sigma_b + \sigma_b'$ contributes $-\frac{\sigma_b + \sigma_b'}{2\epsilon_0}$ at $z = 0^-$ and $+\frac{\sigma_b + \sigma_b'}{2\epsilon_0}$ at $z = 0^+$. Using $\sigma_b = \vec{P}(0^-)\cdot\hat{z} = \epsilon_0 \chi_e E_z(0^-)$ and $\sigma_b' = \vec{P}(0^+)\cdot(-\hat{z}) = -\epsilon_0 \chi_e' E_z(0^+)$:

$$
\sigma_b = \epsilon_0 \chi_e \left[-\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{(q/\epsilon_r')d}{(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{\sigma_b + \sigma_b'}{2\epsilon_0}\right]
$$

$$
\sigma_b' = -\epsilon_0 \chi_e' \left[-\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{(q/\epsilon_r')d}{(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}} + \frac{\sigma_b + \sigma_b'}{2\epsilon_0}\right]
$$

Dividing the first equation by $\chi_e$ and the second by $\chi_e'$, then subtracting eliminates $\frac{\sigma_b + \sigma_b'}{2}$:

$$
\frac{\sigma_b}{\chi_e} - \frac{\sigma_b'}{\chi_e'} = -\frac{qd/\epsilon_r'}{2\pi(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}} \implies \sigma_b' = \chi_e'\left[\frac{\sigma_b}{\chi_e} + \frac{qd/\epsilon_r'}{2\pi(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}}\right]
$$

Substituting $\sigma_b'$ back into the first equation and using $1 + \chi_e' = \epsilon_r'$ and $1 + (\chi_e + \chi_e')/2 = (\epsilon_r + \epsilon_r')/2$:

$$
\sigma_b \left(\frac{\epsilon_r + \epsilon_r'}{2\chi_e}\right) = -\frac{qd}{4\pi(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}} \implies \sigma_b = -\frac{\chi_e}{\epsilon_r + \epsilon_r'}\frac{qd}{2\pi(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}}
$$

and therefore

$$
\sigma_b' = \frac{\chi_e' \epsilon_r}{\epsilon_r'(\epsilon_r + \epsilon_r')}\frac{qd}{2\pi(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}}
$$

The net bound surface charge density at $z = 0$ is

$$
\sigma_t = \sigma_b + \sigma_b' = \frac{\chi_e'\epsilon_r - \chi_e \epsilon_r'}{\epsilon_r'(\epsilon_r + \epsilon_r')}\frac{qd}{2\pi(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\epsilon_r' - \epsilon_r}{\epsilon_r'(\epsilon_r + \epsilon_r')}\frac{qd}{2\pi(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}}
$$

Integrating over the entire $xy$-plane ($\int_0^\infty \frac{d\,2\pi r\,dr}{(r^2+d^2)^{3/2}} = 2\pi$) gives the total surface bound charge:

$$
q_{\text{surf}} = \frac{\epsilon_r' - \epsilon_r}{\epsilon_r'(\epsilon_r + \epsilon_r')}q
$$

Now invoke the method of images:

- **For $z > 0$:** Replace the lower dielectric ($z < 0$) by an image charge $q_{\text{surf}}$ at $(0,0,-d)$, acting alongside $q_t = q/\epsilon_r'$ at $(0,0,d)$:

$$
V(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r'}\left[\frac{q}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z - d)^2}} + \left(\frac{\epsilon_r' - \epsilon_r}{\epsilon_r' + \epsilon_r}\right)\frac{q}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z + d)^2}}\right] \qquad (z > 0)
$$

- **For $z < 0$:** Account for the entire charge in $z \ge 0$ by placing $q_t + q_{\text{surf}} = \frac{2q}{\epsilon_r' + \epsilon_r}$ at $(0,0,d)$:

$$
V(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{2q}{\epsilon_r' + \epsilon_r}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z - d)^2}} \qquad (z < 0)
$$

Direct differentiation confirms that $V$ is continuous at $z = 0$, satisfies $\epsilon_r' \left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\right|_{0^+} = \epsilon_r \left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\right|_{0^-}$, and solves Poisson's equation in both half-spaces, so by uniqueness it is the exact potential everywhere.

</div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="4.26" title="Energy of a Charged Conductor with a Dielectric Shell" tag="Griffiths 4.26 · Electrostatic Energy in Linear Media">
<div slot="problem">

A spherical conductor of radius $a$ carries a free charge $Q$. It is surrounded by linear dielectric material of susceptibility $\chi_e$, out to radius $b$. Find the energy of this configuration using $W = \frac{1}{2}\int \vec{D}\cdot\vec{E}\,d\tau$ (Eq. 4.58).

</div>
<div slot="answer">

$$
W = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)}\left(\frac{1}{a} + \frac{\chi_e}{b}\right)
$$

</div>
<div slot="solution">

From Example 4.5, spherical symmetry gives the displacement $\vec{D}$ and electric field $\vec{E}$ everywhere (with $\epsilon = \epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)$):

$$
\vec{D}(r) = \begin{cases} \vec{0}, & r < a \\ \dfrac{Q}{4\pi r^2}\hat{r}, & r > a \end{cases}, \qquad \vec{E}(r) = \begin{cases} \vec{0}, & r < a \\ \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon r^2}\hat{r}, & a < r < b \\ \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\hat{r}, & r > b \end{cases}
$$

Applying Eq. 4.58, $W = \frac{1}{2}\int \vec{D}\cdot\vec{E}\,d\tau$:

$$
W = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{(4\pi)^2}\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_a^b \frac{1}{r^4}(4\pi r^2\,dr) + \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_b^\infty \frac{1}{r^4}(4\pi r^2\,dr)\right]
$$

$$
W = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi}\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b}\right) + \frac{1}{\epsilon_0 b}\right] = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)}\left[\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1+\chi_e}{b}\right]
$$

$$
W = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)}\left(\frac{1}{a} + \frac{\chi_e}{b}\right)
$$

(As a check, notice that $W = \frac{1}{2}Q V(0)$ using the result of Example 4.5.)

<ProblemCard num="4.27" title="Energy of a Uniformly Polarized Sphere (Electret)" tag="Griffiths 4.27 · Comparison of Energy Formulas">
<div slot="problem">

Calculate $W$, using both $W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$ (Eq. 4.55) and $W = \frac{1}{2}\int \vec{D}\cdot\vec{E}\,d\tau$ (Eq. 4.58), for a sphere of radius $R$ with frozen-in uniform polarization $\vec{P}$ (Example 4.2). Comment on the discrepancy. Which (if either) is the "true" energy of the system?

</div>
<div slot="answer">

- From Eq. 4.55: $W = \dfrac{2\pi R^3 P^2}{9\epsilon_0}$.
- From Eq. 4.58: $W = 0$.
- **Discrepancy:** Eq. 4.58 measures work done on *free* charges in a *linear* dielectric ($\vec{D} = \epsilon\vec{E}$); here there are no free charges and the frozen-in electret is nonlinear. Eq. 4.55 gives the true macroscopic *electrostatic* energy of the bound charge distribution, though it omits the internal mechanical/chemical work required to freeze the dipoles in place.

</div>
<div slot="solution">

From Example 4.2 and Eq. 3.103 (aligning $\vec{P} = P\hat{z}$), the electric field of a uniformly polarized sphere of radius $R$ is

$$
\vec{E} = \begin{cases} -\dfrac{1}{3\epsilon_0}P\hat{z}, & r < R \\[8pt] \dfrac{R^3 P}{3\epsilon_0 r^3}\left(2\cos\theta\,\hat{r} + \sin\theta\,\hat{\theta}\right), & r > R \end{cases}
$$

**1. Using Eq. 4.55 ($W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$):**

Inside the sphere ($r < R$):

$$
W_{r<R} = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\left(\frac{P}{3\epsilon_0}\right)^2 \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\right) = \frac{2\pi R^3 P^2}{27\epsilon_0}
$$

Outside the sphere ($r > R$):

$$
W_{r>R} = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\left(\frac{R^3 P}{3\epsilon_0}\right)^2 \int_0^{2\pi} d\phi \int_0^\pi (4\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta \int_R^\infty \frac{r^2\,dr}{r^6}
$$

Since $4\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1 + 3\cos^2\theta$, the angular integral is $\int_0^\pi (1 + 3\cos^2\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta = \left[-\cos\theta - \cos^3\theta\right]_0^\pi = 4$, and the radial integral is $\frac{1}{3R^3}$. Thus

$$
W_{r>R} = \frac{\pi R^6 P^2}{9\epsilon_0} \cdot 4 \cdot \frac{1}{3R^3} = \frac{4\pi R^3 P^2}{27\epsilon_0}
$$

Summing the two regions gives

$$
W_{(4.55)} = \frac{2\pi R^3 P^2}{27\epsilon_0} + \frac{4\pi R^3 P^2}{27\epsilon_0} = \frac{2\pi R^3 P^2}{9\epsilon_0}
$$

**2. Using Eq. 4.58 ($W = \frac{1}{2}\int \vec{D}\cdot\vec{E}\,d\tau$):**

For $r > R$, $\vec{P} = \vec{0}$ so $\vec{D} = \epsilon_0 \vec{E}$, giving the same exterior contribution $W'_{r>R} = \frac{4\pi R^3 P^2}{27\epsilon_0}$. Inside ($r < R$),

$$
\vec{D} = \epsilon_0 \vec{E} + \vec{P} = -\frac{1}{3}\vec{P} + \vec{P} = \frac{2}{3}\vec{P} = -2\epsilon_0 \vec{E}
$$

Hence $\frac{1}{2}\vec{D}\cdot\vec{E} = -\epsilon_0 E^2 = -2\left(\frac{\epsilon_0}{2} E^2\right)$, so the interior contribution is

$$
W'_{r<R} = -2\left(\frac{2\pi R^3 P^2}{27\epsilon_0}\right) = -\frac{4\pi R^3 P^2}{27\epsilon_0}
$$

Adding the two regions gives

$$
W_{(4.58)} = -\frac{4\pi R^3 P^2}{27\epsilon_0} + \frac{4\pi R^3 P^2}{27\epsilon_0} = 0
$$

**Physical interpretation:** Eq. 4.58 calculates the work done on *free* charges when assembling a *linear* dielectric ($\vec{D} = \epsilon\vec{E}$). Here, $\rho_f = 0$ everywhere and a permanently polarized electret is not a linear medium, so Eq. 4.58 yields $0$ and cannot represent the assembly work. Conversely, Eq. 4.55 gives the true macroscopic *electrostatic* energy of the bound charge configuration, though it excludes whatever non-electrostatic / mechanical work was required to stretch and freeze the molecular dipoles (which depends on how the electret was manufactured).

<ProblemCard num="4.28" title="Dielectric Oil Rising in a Coaxial Capacitor" tag="Griffiths 4.28 · Forces on Dielectrics via Virtual Work">
<div slot="problem">

Two long coaxial cylindrical metal tubes (inner radius $a$, outer radius $b$) stand vertically in a tank of dielectric oil (susceptibility $\chi_e$, mass density $\rho$). The inner tube is maintained at potential $V$, and the outer tube is grounded. To what height $h$ does the oil rise in the space between the tubes?

</div>
<div slot="answer">

$$
h = \frac{\epsilon_0 \chi_e V^2}{\rho (b^2 - a^2) g \ln(b/a)}
$$

</div>
<div slot="solution">

Let the total length of the coaxial tubes above the reservoir level be $\ell$ (with $0 < h < \ell$). The capacitor consists of two sections in parallel at the same potential difference $V$: an oil-filled lower section of length $h$ (permittivity $\epsilon = \epsilon_0 \epsilon_r = \epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)$) and an air-filled upper section of length $\ell - h$ (permittivity $\epsilon_0$).

In a coaxial cylinder of length $L$ and uniform permittivity $\epsilon'$, Gauss's law gives $D = \frac{Q}{2\pi s L}$, $E = \frac{Q}{2\pi \epsilon' s L}$, and $V = \frac{Q}{2\pi \epsilon' L}\ln(b/a)$, so its capacitance is $\frac{2\pi\epsilon' L}{\ln(b/a)}$. Adding the two parallel sections:

$$
C(h) = C_{\text{air}} + C_{\text{oil}} = \frac{2\pi\epsilon_0 (\ell - h)}{\ln(b/a)} + \frac{2\pi\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r h}{\ln(b/a)} = \frac{2\pi\epsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)}\left(\ell + \chi_e h\right)
$$

By Eq. 4.64 (and Eq. 4.67, which gives the same force at constant potential $V$), the upward electrical force pulling the oil into the gap is

$$
F_{\text{elec}} = \frac{1}{2}V^2 \frac{dC}{dh} = \frac{1}{2}V^2 \frac{2\pi\epsilon_0 \chi_e}{\ln(b/a)} = \frac{\pi \epsilon_0 \chi_e V^2}{\ln(b/a)}
$$

At equilibrium, this balances the downward gravitational weight of the raised column of oil of volume $\pi(b^2 - a^2)h$:

$$
F_{\text{grav}} = m g = \rho \pi (b^2 - a^2) h g
$$

Setting $F_{\text{elec}} = F_{\text{grav}}$ and solving for $h$:

$$
\rho \pi (b^2 - a^2) g h = \frac{\pi \epsilon_0 \chi_e V^2}{\ln(b/a)} \implies h = \frac{\epsilon_0 \chi_e V^2}{\rho (b^2 - a^2) g \ln(b/a)}
$$

</div>
</ProblemCard>
