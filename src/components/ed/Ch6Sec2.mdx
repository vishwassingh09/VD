### 6.2 The Field of a Magnetized Object

<div class="ed-theory-box">

#### 6.2.1 Bound Currents

Consider a piece of magnetized matter occupying a volume $V$ bounded by a closed surface $S$, with macroscopic magnetization (magnetic dipole moment per unit volume) $\vec{M}(\vec{r}')$. Each infinitesimal volume element $d\tau'$ carries a dipole moment $d\vec{m} = \vec{M}(\vec{r}')\,d\tau'$. Letting $\vec{\mathcal{R}} = \vec{r} - \vec{r}'$, $\mathcal{R} = |\vec{\mathcal{R}}|$, and $\hat{\mathcal{R}} = \vec{\mathcal{R}}/\mathcal{R}$, the total vector potential produced by the magnetized object is obtained by integrating the pure-dipole vector potential:

$$
\vec{A}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int_V \frac{\vec{M}(\vec{r}') \times \hat{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathcal{R}^2}\, d\tau'.
$$

Using the identity $\nabla'\!\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}}\right) = \frac{\hat{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathcal{R}^2}$ together with the vector product rule $\nabla' \times \left(\frac{\vec{M}}{\mathcal{R}}\right) = \frac{1}{\mathcal{R}}(\nabla' \times \vec{M}) - \vec{M} \times \nabla'\!\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}}\right)$, we integrate by parts and convert the total-curl volume integral into a surface integral via $\int_V (\nabla' \times \vec{F})\,d\tau' = -\oint_S \vec{F} \times d\vec{a}'$:

$$
\vec{A}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int_V \frac{\nabla' \times \vec{M}(\vec{r}')}{\mathcal{R}}\, d\tau' + \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \oint_S \frac{\vec{M}(\vec{r}') \times \hat{n}}{\mathcal{R}}\, da',
$$

where $\hat{n}$ is the outward-pointing unit normal on $S$. This takes the exact form of the vector potential generated by a **bound volume current density** $\vec{J}_b$ throughout $V$ and a **bound surface current density** $\vec{K}_b$ on $S$:

$$
\vec{J}_b = \nabla \times \vec{M}, \qquad \vec{K}_b = \vec{M} \times \hat{n},
$$

so that

$$
\vec{A}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int_V \frac{\vec{J}_b(\vec{r}')}{\mathcal{R}}\, d\tau' + \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \oint_S \frac{\vec{K}_b(\vec{r}')}{\mathcal{R}}\, da'.
$$

Consequently, the magnetic field $\vec{B} = \nabla \times \vec{A}$ of a magnetized object is identical to that produced by $\vec{J}_b$ and $\vec{K}_b$, directly paralleling the bound charges $\rho_b = -\nabla \cdot \vec{P}$ and $\sigma_b = \vec{P} \cdot \hat{n}$ in electrostatics. For example, a sphere of radius $R$ with uniform magnetization $\vec{M} = M\hat{z}$ has $\vec{J}_b = \vec{0}$ and $\vec{K}_b = M\sin\theta\,\hat{\phi}$, equivalent to a uniformly charged spinning spherical shell ($\sigma\omega R \leftrightarrow M$). Its interior field is uniform, $\vec{B}_{\text{in}} = \frac{2}{3}\mu_0\vec{M}$, while its exterior field is that of a pure dipole $\vec{m} = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\vec{M}$.

#### 6.2.2 Physical Interpretation of Bound Currents

Modeling the aligned atomic dipoles as tiny circulating current loops of area $a$, thickness $t$, and current $I$ ($m = Ia = Mat \implies I = Mt$) clarifies the physical origin of $\vec{K}_b$ and $\vec{J}_b$:

- **Surface current ($\vec{K}_b$):** In a uniformly magnetized slab, internal currents of adjacent loops run in opposite directions and cancel pairwise. Along the outer boundary, however, no adjacent loop exists to cancel the edge current, leaving a net macroscopic ribbon of surface current $K_b = I/t = M$, or vectorially $\vec{K}_b = \vec{M} \times \hat{n}$ (which vanishes automatically on faces where $\vec{M} \parallel \hat{n}$). Although no single electron traverses the entire surface, the net macroscopic current is genuine and generates $\vec{B}$ in the same manner as any free current.
- **Volume current ($\vec{J}_b$):** When $\vec{M}$ is nonuniform, adjacent loops carry unequal currents and fail to cancel internally. Comparing adjacent volume elements along $y$ and $z$ yields a net current density $(J_b)_x = \frac{\partial M_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial M_y}{\partial z}$, recovering $\vec{J}_b = \nabla \times \vec{M}$. Moreover, $\nabla \cdot \vec{J}_b = \nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \vec{M}) = 0$ identically, guaranteeing steady-current conservation.

#### 6.2.3 The Magnetic Field Inside Matter

The true microscopic magnetic field $\vec{B}_{\text{micro}}$ inside matter fluctuates wildly over atomic length and time scales. The macroscopic magnetic field $\vec{B}$ is defined as the spatial average of $\vec{B}_{\text{micro}}$ over a region large enough to enclose many atoms (smoothing out atomic graininess just as $\vec{M}$ does) yet small compared to macroscopic scales of variation. Applying the bound-current formulation of Section 6.2.1 directly yields this exact macroscopic field $\vec{B}$ even at points inside the magnetized medium (proven rigorously in Problem 6.11).

<ProblemCard num="6.7" title="Uniformly Magnetized Infinite Cylinder" tag="Griffiths 6.7 · Bound Currents & Solenoid Equivalence">

<div slot="problem">

An infinitely long circular cylinder carries a uniform magnetization $\vec{M}$ parallel to its axis. Find the magnetic field (due to $\vec{M}$) inside and outside the cylinder.

</div>

<div slot="answer">

$$
\vec{B} = \begin{cases} \mu_0 \vec{M} & \text{(inside the cylinder)}, \\[6pt] \vec{0} & \text{(outside the cylinder)}. \end{cases}
$$

</div>

<div slot="solution">

Choose cylindrical coordinates $(s, \phi, z)$ with the $z$-axis along the axis of the cylinder of radius $R$, so that $\vec{M} = M\hat{z}$ inside ($s < R$) and $\vec{M} = \vec{0}$ outside ($s > R$).

**Step 1: Determine the bound currents.**

Inside the cylinder, $\vec{M}$ is spatially constant, so the bound volume current density vanishes:

$$
\vec{J}_b = \nabla \times \vec{M} = \vec{0}.
$$

On the cylindrical boundary $s = R$, the outward unit normal is $\hat{n} = \hat{s}$, giving a bound surface current density

$$
\vec{K}_b = \vec{M} \times \hat{n} = (M\hat{z}) \times \hat{s} = M\hat{\phi}.
$$

**Step 2: Identify the equivalent current configuration and apply Ampère's law.**

The bound surface current $\vec{K}_b = M\hat{\phi}$ at $s = R$ is identical to that of an infinitely long ideal solenoid carrying azimuthal surface current $K = M$ (corresponding to $nI = M$ in standard solenoid notation). By axial and translational symmetry, the magnetic field is purely longitudinal, $\vec{B} = B(s)\hat{z}$, and vanishes at infinity ($B(\infty) = 0$).

- **Outside ($s > R$):** Consider a rectangular Amperian loop of length $L$ parallel to the $z$-axis, with one longitudinal leg at radius $s > R$ and the other at $s \to \infty$. Since no bound current is enclosed ($I_{\text{enc}} = 0$) and $B(\infty) = 0$,

$$
\oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = B(s)L - B(\infty)L = 0 \implies \vec{B}_{\text{out}} = \vec{0}.
$$

- **Inside ($s < R$):** Now place one longitudinal leg of length $L$ inside the cylinder at radius $s < R$ and the other outside ($s > R$), where $B = 0$. The loop encloses surface current $I_{\text{enc}} = K_b L = ML$. Ampère's law gives

$$
B(s)L = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} = \mu_0 M L \implies \vec{B}_{\text{in}} = \mu_0 M\hat{z} = \mu_0\vec{M}.
$$

<ProblemCard num="6.8" title="Cylinder with Azimuthal Magnetization" tag="Griffiths 6.8 · Volume & Surface Bound Currents">

<div slot="problem">

A long circular cylinder of radius $R$ carries a magnetization $\vec{M} = k s^2 \hat{\phi}$, where $k$ is a constant, $s$ is the distance from the axis, and $\hat{\phi}$ is the usual azimuthal unit vector. Find the magnetic field due to $\vec{M}$, for points inside and outside the cylinder.

</div>

<div slot="answer">

$$
\vec{B} = \begin{cases} \mu_0 k s^2 \hat{\phi} = \mu_0 \vec{M} & (s < R), \\[6pt] \vec{0} & (s > R). \end{cases}
$$

</div>

<div slot="solution">

**Step 1: Compute the bound volume and surface currents.**

In cylindrical coordinates $(s, \phi, z)$, the magnetization inside the cylinder ($s < R$) has only an azimuthal component $M_\phi(s) = k s^2$. Using the curl in cylindrical coordinates, the bound volume current density is

$$
\vec{J}_b = \nabla \times \vec{M} = \frac{1}{s}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\!\left(s M_\phi\right)\hat{z} = \frac{1}{s}\frac{d}{ds}\!\left(k s^3\right)\hat{z} = 3 k s\,\hat{z}.
$$

On the cylindrical surface $s = R$, the outward unit normal is $\hat{n} = \hat{s}$, so the bound surface current density is

$$
\vec{K}_b = \vec{M}(R) \times \hat{n} = \left(k R^2 \hat{\phi}\right) \times \hat{s} = -k R^2 \hat{z}.
$$

Thus, the bound volume current flows in the $+\hat{z}$ direction throughout the interior and returns in the $-\hat{z}$ direction along the surface. As a consistency check, the net axial current vanishes:

$$
I_{\text{total}} = \int_0^R (3ks)(2\pi s\,ds) + (-kR^2)(2\pi R) = 2\pi k R^3 - 2\pi k R^3 = 0.
$$

**Step 2: Calculate $\vec{B}$ via Ampère's law.**

Because all currents are purely longitudinal and axially symmetric, the magnetic field is purely circumferential: $\vec{B} = B(s)\hat{\phi}$. Choosing a circular Amperian loop of radius $s$ coaxial with the cylinder:

- **Inside ($s < R$):** The enclosed current comes solely from $\vec{J}_b$:

$$
B(s)(2\pi s) = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} = \mu_0 \int_0^s (3 k s')(2\pi s'\,ds') = 2\pi \mu_0 k s^3 \implies \vec{B}_{\text{in}}(s) = \mu_0 k s^2 \hat{\phi} = \mu_0 \vec{M}.
$$

- **Outside ($s > R$):** The Amperian loop encloses both the volume current and the surface return current, so $I_{\text{enc}} = I_{\text{total}} = 0$. Therefore,

$$
B(s)(2\pi s) = 0 \implies \vec{B}_{\text{out}} = \vec{0}.
$$

*(Note: In both Problems 6.7 and 6.8, there is no free current $\vec{J}_f = \vec{0}$ and the high symmetry forces the auxiliary field $\vec{H} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec{B} - \vec{M}$ to vanish everywhere, immediately yielding $\vec{B} = \mu_0\vec{M}$ inside and $\vec{B} = \vec{0}$ outside.)*

</div>

</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="6.9" title="Finite Magnetized Cylinder vs. Bar Electret" tag="Griffiths 6.9 · Bar Magnet Field Topology">

<div slot="problem">

A short circular cylinder of radius $a$ and length $L$ carries a "frozen-in" uniform magnetization $\vec{M}$ parallel to its axis. Find the bound current, and sketch the magnetic field of the cylinder. (Make three sketches: one for $L \gg a$, one for $L \ll a$, and one for $L \approx a$.) Compare this bar magnet with the bar electret of Problem 4.11.

</div>

<div slot="answer">

- **Bound currents:** $\vec{J}_b = \vec{0}$ inside; $\vec{K}_b = M\hat{\phi}$ on the curved cylindrical wall ($s = a$), and $\vec{K}_b = \vec{0}$ on the top and bottom circular end caps ($z = \pm L/2$).
- **Field sketches:** The configuration is equivalent to a finite solenoid of radius $a$, length $L$, and surface current $K_b = M$. All field lines form closed loops passing upward (parallel to $\vec{M}$) through the interior and returning downward outside.
- **Comparison with the bar electret:** Outside the cylinder, $\vec{B}$ has the exact same field-line geometry as the external electric field $\vec{E}$ of the bar electret (with $\mu_0 M \leftrightarrow P/\epsilon_0$). Inside the cylinder, however, $\vec{B}$ points **parallel** to $\vec{M}$, whereas $\vec{E}$ inside the electret points **antiparallel** to $\vec{P}$.

</div>

<div slot="solution">

**Step 1: Find the bound currents.**

Align the cylinder's axis with the $z$-axis, centered at $z = 0$, so that $\vec{M} = M\hat{z}$ for $s < a$ and $|z| < L/2$. Since $\vec{M}$ is uniform inside the cylinder, the bound volume current density is zero:

$$
\vec{J}_b = \nabla \times \vec{M} = \vec{0}.
$$

For the bound surface current $\vec{K}_b = \vec{M} \times \hat{n}$:
- On the top and bottom circular faces ($z = \pm L/2$), $\hat{n} = \pm \hat{z}$, which is parallel or antiparallel to $\vec{M}$, so $\vec{K}_b = \vec{0}$.
- On the curved lateral surface ($s = a$), $\hat{n} = \hat{s}$, giving

$$
\vec{K}_b = (M\hat{z}) \times \hat{s} = M\hat{\phi}.
$$

Hence, the bar magnet is magnetically equivalent to a finite circular solenoid of radius $a$ and length $L$ carrying uniform azimuthal surface current $K_b = M$.

**Step 2: Describe the magnetic field sketches in the three geometric limits.**

Because $\nabla \cdot \vec{B} = 0$, magnetic field lines are always closed continuous loops:

1. **Long cylinder ($L \gg a$, thin bar magnet / long solenoid):** Deep inside the cylinder ($|z| \ll L/2$), the field is nearly uniform and parallel to $\vec{M}$, with $\vec{B} \approx \mu_0 M\hat{z}$. Near the ends ($z = \pm L/2$), the field weakens to roughly $\frac{1}{2}\mu_0 M\hat{z}$ at the center of each end cap, flares outward from the top ("north") face, loops back very weakly along the exterior, and re-enters through the bottom ("south") face. Far from the magnet ($r \gg L$), the field approaches that of a pure magnetic dipole $\vec{m} = \pi a^2 L \vec{M}$.
2. **Flat disk ($L \ll a$, thin magnetized wafer):** Here the surface current acts like a single circular loop of radius $a$ carrying total current $I = K_b L = M L$. Field lines wrap tightly in small loops around the thin rim at $s = a$, passing upward ($+\hat{z}$) through the disk interior and returning downward ($-\hat{z}$) outside $s > a$. At the center ($s = 0, z = 0$), the field is weak: $B(0) \approx \frac{\mu_0 I}{2a} = \frac{\mu_0 M L}{2a} \ll \mu_0 M$. Far away ($r \gg a$), it again takes the dipole form for $\vec{m} = \pi a^2 L \vec{M}$.
3. **Intermediate cylinder ($L \approx a$, stubby bar magnet):** On the axis at the center ($z = 0$), integration of the finite solenoid yields $B(0) = \mu_0 M \frac{L}{\sqrt{L^2 + 4a^2}}$ pointing along $+\hat{z}$. Field lines bow outward across the end faces and through the ends of the curved wall, looping back externally from top to bottom as an intermediate physical dipole pattern.

**Step 3: Comparison with the bar electret (Problem 4.11).**

A bar electret of the same dimensions with uniform frozen polarization $\vec{P} = P\hat{z}$ has $\rho_b = 0$ and bound surface charges $\sigma_b = \pm P$ on the top and bottom end faces ($z = \pm L/2$).
- **Exterior region:** Outside the bar magnet, $\nabla \times \vec{B} = \vec{0}$ and $\nabla \cdot \vec{B} = 0$, while the discontinuity in the normal component of $\vec{H} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec{B} - \vec{M}$ at the end caps mimics effective "magnetic surface charges" $\sigma_m = \pm M$. Thus, the external magnetic field $\vec{B}_{\text{out}} = \mu_0 \vec{H}_{\text{out}}$ has the exact same spatial form as the external electric field $\vec{E}_{\text{out}}$ of the bar electret (under the replacement $\mu_0 M \leftrightarrow P/\epsilon_0$).
- **Interior region:** Inside the bar, the two fields point in **opposite** directions. In the bar electret, field lines originate on $+\sigma_b$ at $z = +L/2$ and terminate on $-\sigma_b$ at $z = -L/2$, so $\vec{E}_{\text{in}}$ points **antiparallel** to $\vec{P}$ (with $E_{\text{in}} \approx -P/\epsilon_0$ for $L \ll a$ and $E_{\text{in}} \to 0$ for $L \gg a$). In the bar magnet, closed loops require $\vec{B}_{\text{in}}$ to point from $z = -L/2$ to $z = +L/2$, **parallel** to $\vec{M}$. (Instead, it is the auxiliary field $\vec{H} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec{B} - \vec{M}$ that points opposite to $\vec{M}$ inside, serving as the true mathematical counterpart to $\vec{E}$.)

</div>

<ProblemCard num="6.10" title="Magnetized Iron Ring with a Narrow Gap" tag="Griffiths 6.10 · Superposition & Gap Field">

<div slot="problem">

An iron rod of length $L$ and square cross section (side $a$) is given a uniform longitudinal magnetization $\vec{M}$, and then bent around into a circle with a narrow gap (width $w$), as shown in Fig. 6.14. Find the magnetic field at the center of the gap, assuming $w \ll a \ll L$. *(Hint: treat it as the superposition of a complete torus plus a square loop with reversed current.)*

</div>

<div slot="answer">

$$
\vec{B} = \mu_0 \vec{M} \left(1 - \frac{2\sqrt{2}\, w}{\pi a}\right)
$$

(pointing across the gap in the direction of $\vec{M}$).

</div>

<div slot="solution">

**Step 1: Decompose the system by superposition.**

Let the ring have mean circumference $L$ and mean radius $R_0 \approx L/(2\pi) \gg a$. Because the magnetization $\vec{M}$ is longitudinal (azimuthal around the ring, $\vec{M} = M\hat{\phi}$) and uniform in magnitude, the bound surface current circulating around the square cross section of the iron rod is $K_b = |\vec{M} \times \hat{n}| = M$. On the two flat end faces bounding the gap, $\vec{M}$ is parallel/antiparallel to $\hat{n}$, so there is no bound surface current on the gap faces.

In the gap of width $w$, the iron—and therefore its surface current ribbon of width $w$—is absent. By linearity of magnetostatics, the magnetic field of the gapped ring equals the superposition of:
1. A **complete, gapless magnetized torus** carrying its full bound currents, plus
2. A **square current loop** of side $a$ placed at the gap, carrying the missing surface current $I = K_b w = M w$ in the **reversed** direction (canceling the torus surface current over the width $w$). Because $w \ll a$, this missing ribbon acts as a filamentary square loop; moreover, any volume current $\vec{J}_b = \nabla \times (M\hat{\phi}) = \frac{M}{s}\hat{z}$ inside the gap slice contributes a net current $\sim \frac{M}{R_0}aw \sim Mw\left(\frac{a}{L}\right) \ll Mw$ since $a \ll L$, which is negligible.

**Step 2: Field of the complete torus.**

For a complete azimuthally magnetized torus with $\vec{M} = M\hat{\phi}$ (and no free current, $\vec{J}_f = \vec{0}$), azimuthal symmetry and Ampère's law for $\vec{H}$ imply $\vec{H}_{\text{torus}} = \vec{0}$ everywhere (equivalently, summing the inner wall current $K_b = M$ and volume current $J_b = M/s$ in Ampère's law for $\vec{B}$ yields $B(2\pi s) = \mu_0[2\pi M R_{\text{in}} + 2\pi M(s - R_{\text{in}})] = 2\pi\mu_0 M s$). Thus, inside the torus cross section,

$$
\vec{B}_{\text{torus}} = \mu_0 \vec{M}.
$$

**Step 3: Field at the center of the reversed square loop.**

By the Biot–Savart law, a straight wire segment of length $a$ carrying current $I$ produces at a perpendicular distance $d = a/2$ along its perpendicular bisector (subtending angles from $-45^\circ$ to $+45^\circ$) a field of magnitude

$$
B_{\text{seg}} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi (a/2)}\left(\sin 45^\circ - \sin(-45^\circ)\right) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi a}.
$$

Summing the four equal sides of the square loop gives the field at its center:

$$
B_{\text{sq}} = 4 B_{\text{seg}} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}\,\mu_0 I}{\pi a}.
$$

Substituting the reversed current $I = M w$, and noting that $\vec{B}_{\text{sq}}$ points opposite to $\vec{B}_{\text{torus}}$, we obtain

$$
B_{\text{sq}} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}\,\mu_0 M w}{\pi a}.
$$

**Step 4: Combine both contributions.**

Superposing the two fields at the center of the gap yields

$$
\vec{B} = \vec{B}_{\text{torus}} + \vec{B}_{\text{sq}} = \mu_0 \vec{M}\left(1 - \frac{2\sqrt{2}\,w}{\pi a}\right).
$$

<ProblemCard num="6.11" title="Macroscopic Field Inside Magnetized Matter" tag="Griffiths 6.11 · Spatial Averaging Proof">

<div slot="problem">

In Section 6.2.1, we began with the potential of a perfect dipole (Eq. 6.10), whereas in fact we are dealing with physical dipoles. Show, by the method of Section 4.2.3, that we nonetheless get the correct macroscopic field.

</div>

<div slot="answer">

Averaging $\vec{B}_{\text{micro}}$ over a macroscopically small, microscopically large sphere $\mathcal{S}$ of radius $R$ centered at $\vec{r}$ splits $\vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \vec{B}_{\text{out}}(\vec{r}) + \vec{B}_{\text{in}}(\vec{r})$. By the mean-value theorem for harmonic functions, $\vec{B}_{\text{out}}$ equals the field at $\vec{r}$ of pure dipoles outside $\mathcal{S}$. Inside $\mathcal{S}$, the average field of all physical dipoles is $\vec{B}_{\text{in}} = \frac{2}{3}\mu_0\vec{M}(\vec{r})$, which identically matches the field at $\vec{r}$ of a uniformly magnetized sphere $\mathcal{S}$ computed from pure dipoles (Example 6.1). Thus the bound-current integral over the entire volume reproduces the exact macroscopic field.

</div>

<div slot="solution">

**Step 1: Define the macroscopic field via spatial averaging.**

Let $\vec{B}_{\text{micro}}(\vec{r}')$ be the true microscopic magnetic field generated by all atomic physical dipoles (microscopic steady current loops) in the material. The macroscopic magnetic field $\vec{B}(\vec{r})$ at a point $\vec{r}$ inside the medium is defined as the volume average of $\vec{B}_{\text{micro}}$ over a sphere $\mathcal{S}$ of radius $R$ centered at $\vec{r}$, where $R$ is much larger than atomic dimensions (containing many atoms) yet much smaller than the scale over which $\vec{M}$ varies appreciably:

$$
\vec{B}(\vec{r}) \equiv \frac{1}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3} \int_{\mathcal{S}} \vec{B}_{\text{micro}}(\vec{r}')\, d\tau'.
$$

By superposition, divide the sources into all dipoles **outside** $\mathcal{S}$ and all dipoles **inside** $\mathcal{S}$:

$$
\vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \vec{B}_{\text{out}}(\vec{r}) + \vec{B}_{\text{in}}(\vec{r}).
$$

**Step 2: Evaluate the exterior contribution $\vec{B}_{\text{out}}(\vec{r})$.**

Every dipole outside $\mathcal{S}$ lies far (on atomic scales) from the interior of $\mathcal{S}$ (except for an insignificant boundary shell of thickness $\sim d_{\text{atom}} \ll R$), so its field inside $\mathcal{S}$ is accurately that of a **pure** magnetic dipole. Furthermore, inside $\mathcal{S}$ there are no exterior sources, so $\nabla \cdot \vec{B}_{\text{out, micro}} = 0$ and $\nabla \times \vec{B}_{\text{out, micro}} = \vec{0}$. Taking the curl of the curl shows that each Cartesian component of $\vec{B}_{\text{out, micro}}$ satisfies Laplace's equation ($\nabla^2 B_k = 0$) throughout $\mathcal{S}$. By the mean-value theorem for harmonic functions, the average of $\vec{B}_{\text{out, micro}}$ over the sphere $\mathcal{S}$ equals its exact value at the center $\vec{r}$. Therefore, $\vec{B}_{\text{out}}(\vec{r})$ is precisely the field at $\vec{r}$ produced by all pure dipoles in the exterior volume $V - \mathcal{S}$:

$$
\vec{B}_{\text{out}}(\vec{r}) = \nabla \times \left[ \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int_{V - \mathcal{S}} \frac{\vec{M}(\vec{r}') \times \hat{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathcal{R}^2}\, d\tau' \right].
$$

**Step 3: Evaluate the interior contribution $\vec{B}_{\text{in}}(\vec{r})$.**

Now choose the origin at the center $\vec{r}$ of $\mathcal{S}$ and compute the average magnetic field over $\mathcal{S}$ produced by any steady microscopic current density $\vec{J}(\vec{r}'')$ confined inside $\mathcal{S}$ (representing all physical dipoles within $\mathcal{S}$). From the Biot–Savart law,

$$
\int_{\mathcal{S}} \vec{B}_{\text{in, micro}}(\vec{r}')\, d\tau' = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int_{\mathcal{S}} \vec{J}(\vec{r}'') \times \left[ \int_{\mathcal{S}} \frac{\vec{r}' - \vec{r}''}{|\vec{r}' - \vec{r}''|^3}\, d\tau' \right] d\tau''.
$$

The bracketed integral is minus the electrostatic field at $\vec{r}'' \in \mathcal{S}$ of a uniformly charged sphere of density $\rho = 1$ (with $4\pi\epsilon_0 \to 1$), which by Gauss's law equals $-\frac{4\pi}{3}\vec{r}''$. Substituting this into the integral gives

$$
\int_{\mathcal{S}} \vec{B}_{\text{in, micro}}(\vec{r}')\, d\tau' = \frac{\mu_0}{3} \int_{\mathcal{S}} \left(\vec{r}'' \times \vec{J}(\vec{r}'')\right) d\tau'' = \frac{2}{3}\mu_0 \vec{m}_{\text{total}},
$$

since the total magnetic dipole moment inside $\mathcal{S}$ is $\vec{m}_{\text{total}} = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{S}} (\vec{r}'' \times \vec{J}(\vec{r}''))\,d\tau''$ (recovering Griffiths Eq. 5.93; the same result holds for intrinsic point/spin dipoles via the contact term $\frac{2}{3}\mu_0 \vec{m}\,\delta^3(\vec{r}')$ in Eq. 5.94).

Because $\mathcal{S}$ is macroscopically small, the magnetization across $\mathcal{S}$ is essentially uniform with value $\vec{M}(\vec{r})$, so $\vec{m}_{\text{total}} = \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\right)\vec{M}(\vec{r})$. Dividing by the volume of $\mathcal{S}$ yields

$$
\vec{B}_{\text{in}}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3} \left(\frac{2}{3}\mu_0 \vec{m}_{\text{total}}\right) = \frac{2}{3}\mu_0 \vec{M}(\vec{r}).
$$

**Step 4: Recombine with the pure-dipole formalism.**

By Example 6.1 (Eq. 6.16), a sphere $\mathcal{S}$ with uniform magnetization $\vec{M}(\vec{r})$ treated by the pure-dipole (bound-current) method of Section 6.2.1 produces at its center $\vec{r}$ the exact same field $\frac{2}{3}\mu_0\vec{M}(\vec{r})$. Hence, $\vec{B}_{\text{in}}(\vec{r})$ equals the pure-dipole field integrated over $\mathcal{S}$, and summing $\vec{B}_{\text{out}}(\vec{r}) + \vec{B}_{\text{in}}(\vec{r})$ reconstructs the pure-dipole integral of Eq. 6.11 (and therefore the bound-current expressions $\vec{J}_b = \nabla \times \vec{M}$ and $\vec{K}_b = \vec{M} \times \hat{n}$) over the entire volume $V$.
