### 1.2 Differential Calculus

<div class="ed-theory-box">

**Core Theory (Griffiths §§1.2.1–1.2.7 — Gradient, Del, Divergence, Curl, Product Rules & Second Derivatives):**

- **Ordinary Derivatives & Total Differential in 3D:** For a single-variable function $f(x)$, $df = (df/dx)\,dx$ gives the proportionality factor relating an infinitesimal increment $dx$ to the change $df$ (geometrically, the slope of $f$ vs. $x$). For a scalar field $T(x,y,z)$ in three dimensions, the directional rate of change depends on the displacement direction, yet three partial derivatives completely determine the total differential:

$$
dT = \frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\,dx + \frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\,dy + \frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\,dz = (\nabla T)\cdot d\vec{l}
$$

  where $d\vec{l} = dx\,\hat{x} + dy\,\hat{y} + dz\,\hat{z}$.

- **The Gradient ($\nabla T$):** Defined in Cartesian coordinates as the vector field:

$$
\nabla T \equiv \frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\,\hat{x} + \frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\,\hat{y} + \frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\,\hat{z}
$$

  Since $dT = |\nabla T|\,|d\vec{l}|\cos\theta$ (where $\theta$ is the angle between $\nabla T$ and $d\vec{l}$), holding $|d\vec{l}|$ fixed maximizes $dT$ at $\theta = 0$ and yields $dT = 0$ at $\theta = 90^\circ$. Consequently:
  1. $\nabla T$ points in the direction of **steepest ascent** (maximum rate of increase) of $T$, and is perpendicular to contour lines / level surfaces ($T = \text{const}$).
  2. $|\nabla T|$ gives the **maximum spatial rate of increase** (slope along the direction of steepest ascent).
  3. Points where $\nabla T = \mathbf{0}$ are **stationary points** (local maxima, minima, saddle points, or shoulders), assuming $T$ is differentiable (no kinks).

- **The Del Operator ($\nabla$):** Writing unit vectors to the left so derivatives act only on what follows, del is the vector differential operator:

$$
\nabla = \hat{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat{y}\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
$$

  Del is *not* an ordinary vector—it is an instruction to differentiate what stands to its right—yet it mimics ordinary vector algebra in three fundamental operations: the **gradient** $\nabla T$ (on a scalar), the **divergence** $\nabla\cdot\vec{v}$ (dot product with a vector), and the **curl** $\nabla\times\vec{v}$ (cross product with a vector).

- **The Divergence ($\nabla\cdot\vec{v}$):** Acting on a vector function $\vec{v}(x,y,z)$ produces a scalar field:

$$
\nabla\cdot\vec{v} = \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}
$$

  Geometrically, $\nabla\cdot\vec{v}$ measures how strongly $\vec{v}$ **spreads out** from a point: positive divergence corresponds to a local source ("faucet") and negative divergence to a local sink ("drain"). *(Caveat: singular fields such as $\hat{r}/r^2$ can satisfy $\nabla\cdot\vec{v}=0$ everywhere except $r=0$, where the naive derivative breaks down and a Dirac delta contribution resides; see §1.5.)*

- **The Curl ($\nabla\times\vec{v}$):** Acting on a vector function $\vec{v}(x,y,z)$ produces a vector field:

$$
\nabla\times\vec{v} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{x} & \hat{y} & \hat{z} \\ \partial/\partial x & \partial/\partial y & \partial/\partial z \\ v_x & v_y & v_z \end{vmatrix} = \left(\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial z}\right)\hat{x} + \left(\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial x}\right)\hat{y} + \left(\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}\right)\hat{z}
$$

  Geometrically, $\nabla\times\vec{v}$ measures how much $\vec{v}$ **swirls** (circulates) around a point—a small floating paddlewheel placed at a point of nonzero curl rotates, with axis and sense given by the right-hand rule.

- **Linearity, Product Rules, and Quotient Rules:** Gradient, divergence, and curl are all linear operators. Because products of scalar and vector fields yield two scalars ($fg$, $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}$) and two vectors ($f\vec{A}$, $\vec{A}\times\vec{B}$), there are **six product rules**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{(i)}\quad \nabla(fg) &= f\nabla g + g\nabla f \\
\text{(ii)}\quad \nabla(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}) &= \vec{A}\times(\nabla\times\vec{B}) + \vec{B}\times(\nabla\times\vec{A}) + (\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B} + (\vec{B}\cdot\nabla)\vec{A} \\
\text{(iii)}\quad \nabla\cdot(f\vec{A}) &= f(\nabla\cdot\vec{A}) + \vec{A}\cdot(\nabla f) \\
\text{(iv)}\quad \nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) &= \vec{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{A}) - \vec{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{B}) \\
\text{(v)}\quad \nabla\times(f\vec{A}) &= f(\nabla\times\vec{A}) - \vec{A}\times(\nabla f) \\
\text{(vi)}\quad \nabla\times(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) &= (\vec{B}\cdot\nabla)\vec{A} - (\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B} + \vec{A}(\nabla\cdot\vec{B}) - \vec{B}(\nabla\cdot\vec{A})
\end{aligned}
$$

  with corresponding quotient rules ($g \neq 0$):

$$
\nabla\left(\frac{f}{g}\right) = \frac{g\nabla f - f\nabla g}{g^2}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\vec{A}}{g}\right) = \frac{g(\nabla\cdot\vec{A}) - \vec{A}\cdot(\nabla g)}{g^2}, \qquad \nabla\times\left(\frac{\vec{A}}{g}\right) = \frac{g(\nabla\times\vec{A}) + \vec{A}\times(\nabla g)}{g^2}
$$

- **Second Derivatives:** Applying $\nabla$ twice yields five combinations, which reduce to two independent operators and two fundamental identities (valid for $C^2$ fields, relying on the equality of mixed partial derivatives $\partial_x\partial_y = \partial_y\partial_x$, *not* naive algebraic identities with $\nabla$ treated as a vector):
  1. **Laplacian of a scalar:** $\nabla^2 T \equiv \nabla\cdot(\nabla T) = \dfrac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 T}{\partial y^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 T}{\partial z^2}$.
  2. **Curl of a gradient vanishes identically:** $\nabla\times(\nabla T) = \mathbf{0}$. *(Contrast with $(\nabla T)\times(\nabla S)$, which is generally nonzero!)*
  3. **Gradient of a divergence:** $\nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec{v})$ (distinct from the vector Laplacian: $\nabla^2\vec{v} \neq \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec{v})$).
  4. **Divergence of a curl vanishes identically:** $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{v}) = 0$.
  5. **Curl of a curl & Vector Laplacian:**

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec{v}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec{v}) - \nabla^2\vec{v}
$$

  In Cartesian coordinates, $\nabla^2\vec{v} = (\nabla^2 v_x)\hat{x} + (\nabla^2 v_y)\hat{y} + (\nabla^2 v_z)\hat{z}$; in curvilinear coordinates (where basis vectors vary with position), $\nabla^2\vec{v} \equiv \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec{v}) - \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec{v})$ serves as the coordinate-independent definition.

</div>

<ProblemCard num="1.11" title="Computing Gradients of Scalar Functions" tag="Griffiths 1.11 · Gradient">
  <div slot="problem">
    Find the gradients of the following functions:
    1. $f(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^3 + z^4$.
    2. $f(x, y, z) = x^2 y^3 z^4$.
    3. $f(x, y, z) = e^x \sin(y) \ln(z)$.
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    1. $\nabla f = 2x\,\hat{x} + 3y^2\,\hat{y} + 4z^3\,\hat{z}$
    2. $\nabla f = 2xy^3 z^4\,\hat{x} + 3x^2 y^2 z^4\,\hat{y} + 4x^2 y^3 z^3\,\hat{z}$
    3. $\nabla f = e^x \sin(y)\ln(z)\,\hat{x} + e^x \cos(y)\ln(z)\,\hat{y} + \dfrac{e^x \sin(y)}{z}\,\hat{z}$
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    Using the Cartesian gradient definition $\nabla f = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\hat{x} + \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\hat{y} + \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial z}\hat{z}$:

    1. Differentiating $f(x,y,z) = x^2 + y^3 + z^4$ with respect to each coordinate while holding the other two fixed:

    $$
    \nabla f = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2)\,\hat{x} + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y^3)\,\hat{y} + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(z^4)\,\hat{z} = 2x\,\hat{x} + 3y^2\,\hat{y} + 4z^3\,\hat{z}
    $$

    2. Differentiating $f(x,y,z) = x^2 y^3 z^4$:

    $$
    \nabla f = (2x)y^3 z^4\,\hat{x} + x^2(3y^2)z^4\,\hat{y} + x^2 y^3(4z^3)\,\hat{z} = 2xy^3 z^4\,\hat{x} + 3x^2 y^2 z^4\,\hat{y} + 4x^2 y^3 z^3\,\hat{z}
    $$

    3. Differentiating $f(x,y,z) = e^x \sin(y) \ln(z)$ ($z &gt; 0$):

    $$
    \nabla f = e^x \sin(y) \ln(z)\,\hat{x} + e^x \cos(y) \ln(z)\,\hat{y} + \frac{e^x \sin(y)}{z}\,\hat{z}
    $$
  </div>

<ProblemCard num="1.12" title="Topography of a Hill via the Gradient" tag="Griffiths 1.12 · Gradient Geometry">
  <div slot="problem">
    The height of a certain hill (in feet) is given by

    $$
    h(x, y) = 10(2xy - 3x^2 - 4y^2 - 18x + 28y + 12),
    $$

    where $y$ is the distance (in miles) north, $x$ the distance east of South Hadley.
    1. Where is the top of the hill located?
    2. How high is the hill?
    3. How steep is the slope (in feet per mile) at a point $1\text{ mile}$ north and $1\text{ mile}$ east of South Hadley? In what direction is the slope steepest, at that point?
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    1. $x = -2\text{ miles}$, $y = 3\text{ miles}$ (i.e., $3\text{ miles}$ north and $2\text{ miles}$ west of South Hadley).
    2. $h_{\max} = 720\text{ ft}$.
    3. Slope $|\nabla h| = 220\sqrt{2} \approx 311\text{ ft/mile}$, steepest toward the **northwest** (along $\frac{-\hat{x} + \hat{y}}{\sqrt{2}}$).
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    1. **Summit location:** First compute the gradient of $h(x,y)$:

    $$
    \nabla h = \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\hat{x} + \frac{\partial h}{\partial y}\hat{y} = 10\left[(2y - 6x - 18)\hat{x} + (2x - 8y + 28)\hat{y}\right]
    $$

    At the top of the hill, $\nabla h = \mathbf{0}$, giving the linear system:

    $$
    \begin{cases}
    2y - 6x - 18 = 0 \\
    2x - 8y + 28 = 0
    \end{cases}
    $$

    Multiplying the second equation by $3$ gives $6x - 24y + 84 = 0$. Adding this to the first equation eliminates $x$:

    $$
    -22y + 66 = 0 \implies y = 3\text{ miles}
    $$

    Substituting $y = 3$ into $2x - 8y + 28 = 0$ yields $2x - 24 + 28 = 0 \implies x = -2\text{ miles}$. Thus the summit is **$3\text{ miles}$ north and $2\text{ miles}$ west of South Hadley**. (The Hessian $h_{xx} = -60 &lt; 0$, $h_{xx}h_{yy} - h_{xy}^2 = (-60)(-80) - 20^2 = 4400 &gt; 0$ confirms this is indeed a local maximum.)

    2. **Height of the hill:** Evaluate $h$ at $(x,y) = (-2, 3)$:

    $$
    h(-2, 3) = 10\left[2(-2)(3) - 3(-2)^2 - 4(3)^2 - 18(-2) + 28(3) + 12\right] = 10(-12 - 12 - 36 + 36 + 84 + 12) = 720\text{ ft}
    $$

    3. **Slope and direction at $(1, 1)$:** Evaluate $\nabla h$ at $x = 1$, $y = 1$:

    $$
    \nabla h(1, 1) = 10\left[(2 - 6 - 18)\hat{x} + (2 - 8 + 28)\hat{y}\right] = 10(-22\hat{x} + 22\hat{y}) = 220(-\hat{x} + \hat{y})\text{ ft/mile}
    $$

    The steepness (maximum rate of ascent) is its magnitude:

    $$
    |\nabla h(1, 1)| = 220\sqrt{(-1)^2 + 1^2} = 220\sqrt{2} \approx 311\text{ ft/mile}
    $$

    Because $\nabla h(1,1)$ points along $-\hat{x} + \hat{y}$ (west and equally north), the slope is steepest in the **northwest** direction.
  </div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="1.13" title="Gradients of Powers of the Separation Distance" tag="Griffiths 1.13 · Separation Vector">
  <div slot="problem">
    Let $\vec{\mathscr{r}}$ be the separation vector from a fixed point $(x', y', z')$ to the point $(x, y, z)$, and let $\mathscr{r}$ be its length. Show that:
    1. $\nabla(\mathscr{r}^2) = 2\vec{\mathscr{r}}$.
    2. $\nabla(1/\mathscr{r}) = -\hat{\mathscr{r}}/\mathscr{r}^2$.
    3. What is the general formula for $\nabla(\mathscr{r}^n)$?
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    1. $\nabla(\mathscr{r}^2) = 2\vec{\mathscr{r}}$.
    2. $\nabla(1/\mathscr{r}) = -\hat{\mathscr{r}}/\mathscr{r}^2$.
    3. $\nabla(\mathscr{r}^n) = n\mathscr{r}^{n-1}\hat{\mathscr{r}}$ (for any real $n$, valid for $\mathscr{r} &gt; 0$ when $n \le 1$).
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    With the fixed point $(x',y',z')$, the separation vector and its length are:

    $$
    \vec{\mathscr{r}} = (x - x')\hat{x} + (y - y')\hat{y} + (z - z')\hat{z}, \qquad \mathscr{r} = \sqrt{(x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2 + (z - z')^2}
    $$

    1. **Gradient of $\mathscr{r}^2$:** Since $\mathscr{r}^2 = (x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2 + (z - z')^2$:

    $$
    \nabla(\mathscr{r}^2) = \frac{\partial(\mathscr{r}^2)}{\partial x}\hat{x} + \frac{\partial(\mathscr{r}^2)}{\partial y}\hat{y} + \frac{\partial(\mathscr{r}^2)}{\partial z}\hat{z} = 2(x - x')\hat{x} + 2(y - y')\hat{y} + 2(z - z')\hat{z} = 2\vec{\mathscr{r}}
    $$

    2. **Gradient of $1/\mathscr{r}$:** Write $\mathscr{r}^{-1} = \left[(x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2 + (z - z')^2\right]^{-1/2}$. Differentiating the $x$-component:

    $$
    \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{r}}\right) = -\frac{1}{2}\left[(x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2 + (z - z')^2\right]^{-3/2}\cdot 2(x - x') = -\frac{x - x'}{\mathscr{r}^3}
    $$

    By symmetry, the $y$- and $z$-components have the same form, so:

    $$
    \nabla\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{r}}\right) = -\frac{(x - x')\hat{x} + (y - y')\hat{y} + (z - z')\hat{z}}{\mathscr{r}^3} = -\frac{\vec{\mathscr{r}}}{\mathscr{r}^3} = -\frac{\hat{\mathscr{r}}}{\mathscr{r}^2}
    $$

    3. **General power $\mathscr{r}^n$:** First note that $\dfrac{\partial \mathscr{r}}{\partial x} = \dfrac{1}{2\mathscr{r}}\cdot 2(x - x') = \dfrac{x - x'}{\mathscr{r}} = \hat{\mathscr{r}}_x$, and similarly for $y$ and $z$, so $\nabla\mathscr{r} = \hat{\mathscr{r}}$. By the chain rule on each component:

    $$
    \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\mathscr{r}^n) = n\mathscr{r}^{n-1}\frac{\partial \mathscr{r}}{\partial x} = n\mathscr{r}^{n-1}\hat{\mathscr{r}}_x \implies \nabla(\mathscr{r}^n) = n\mathscr{r}^{n-1}\hat{\mathscr{r}}
    $$

    Setting $n = 2$ gives $2\mathscr{r}\hat{\mathscr{r}} = 2\vec{\mathscr{r}}$, and $n = -1$ gives $-\mathscr{r}^{-2}\hat{\mathscr{r}}$, recovering parts 1 and 2.
  </div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="1.14" title="Transformation of the Gradient Under Rotations" tag="Griffiths 1.14 · Vector Transformation">
  <div slot="problem">
    Suppose that $f$ is a function of two variables ($y$ and $z$) only. Show that the gradient $\nabla f = (\partial f/\partial y)\hat{y} + (\partial f/\partial z)\hat{z}$ transforms as a vector under rotations, Eq. 1.29.

    *[Hint: $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial \bar{y}} = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial \bar{y}} + \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial z}\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial \bar{y}}$, and the analogous formula for $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial \bar{z}}$. We know that $\bar{y} = y\cos\phi + z\sin\phi$ and $\bar{z} = -y\sin\phi + z\cos\phi$; "solve" these equations for $y$ and $z$ (as functions of $\bar{y}$ and $\bar{z}$), and compute the needed derivatives $\partial y/\partial \bar{y}$, $\partial z/\partial \bar{y}$, etc.]*
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    $(\bar{\nabla} f)_y = \cos\phi\,(\nabla f)_y + \sin\phi\,(\nabla f)_z$ and $(\bar{\nabla} f)_z = -\sin\phi\,(\nabla f)_y + \cos\phi\,(\nabla f)_z$, which matches the vector transformation law (Eq. 1.29).
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    Start from the rotation equations (Griffiths Eq. 1.29):

    $$
    \bar{y} = y\cos\phi + z\sin\phi, \qquad \bar{z} = -y\sin\phi + z\cos\phi
    $$

    To invert for $y$ and $z$ in terms of $\bar{y}$ and $\bar{z}$:
    - Multiply the first by $\sin\phi$ and the second by $\cos\phi$:
      $\bar{y}\sin\phi = y\sin\phi\cos\phi + z\sin^2\phi$ and $\bar{z}\cos\phi = -y\sin\phi\cos\phi + z\cos^2\phi$.
      Adding gives $z(\sin^2\phi + \cos^2\phi) = \bar{y}\sin\phi + \bar{z}\cos\phi \implies z = \bar{y}\sin\phi + \bar{z}\cos\phi$.
    - Similarly, multiplying the first by $\cos\phi$ and the second by $-\sin\phi$ and adding gives $y = \bar{y}\cos\phi - \bar{z}\sin\phi$.

    Computing the four partial derivatives of the inverse transformation:

    $$
    \frac{\partial y}{\partial \bar{y}} = \cos\phi, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial \bar{y}} = \sin\phi, \qquad \frac{\partial y}{\partial \bar{z}} = -\sin\phi, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial \bar{z}} = \cos\phi
    $$

    Now apply the chain rule to obtain the gradient components in the rotated frame:

    $$
    (\bar{\nabla} f)_y = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \bar{y}} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial \bar{y}} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\frac{\partial z}{\partial \bar{y}} = \cos\phi\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} + \sin\phi\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = \cos\phi\,(\nabla f)_y + \sin\phi\,(\nabla f)_z
    $$

    $$
    (\bar{\nabla} f)_z = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \bar{z}} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial \bar{z}} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\frac{\partial z}{\partial \bar{z}} = -\sin\phi\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} + \cos\phi\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = -\sin\phi\,(\nabla f)_y + \cos\phi\,(\nabla f)_z
    $$

    These are precisely the transformation equations of a 2D vector (Eq. 1.29), proving that $\nabla f$ transforms as a true vector.
  </div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="1.15" title="Computing Divergences of Vector Fields" tag="Griffiths 1.15 · Divergence">
  <div slot="problem">
    Calculate the divergence of the following vector functions:
    1. $\vec{v}_a = x^2\hat{x} + 3xz^2\hat{y} - 2xz\hat{z}$.
    2. $\vec{v}_b = xy\hat{x} + 2yz\hat{y} + 3zx\hat{z}$.
    3. $\vec{v}_c = y^2\hat{x} + (2xy + z^2)\hat{y} + 2yz\hat{z}$.
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    1. $\nabla\cdot\vec{v}_a = 0$
    2. $\nabla\cdot\vec{v}_b = 3x + y + 2z$
    3. $\nabla\cdot\vec{v}_c = 2(x + y)$
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    Using $\nabla\cdot\vec{v} = \dfrac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \dfrac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}$:

    1. For $\vec{v}_a = x^2\hat{x} + 3xz^2\hat{y} - 2xz\hat{z}$:

    $$
    \nabla\cdot\vec{v}_a = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(3xz^2) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(-2xz) = 2x + 0 - 2x = 0
    $$

    2. For $\vec{v}_b = xy\hat{x} + 2yz\hat{y} + 3zx\hat{z}$:

    $$
    \nabla\cdot\vec{v}_b = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xy) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2yz) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(3zx) = y + 2z + 3x
    $$

    3. For $\vec{v}_c = y^2\hat{x} + (2xy + z^2)\hat{y} + 2yz\hat{z}$:

    $$
    \nabla\cdot\vec{v}_c = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(y^2) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2xy + z^2) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(2yz) = 0 + 2x + 2y = 2(x + y)
    $$
  </div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="1.16" title="Divergence of the Inverse-Square Radial Field" tag="Griffiths 1.16 · Singular Divergence">
  <div slot="problem">
    Sketch the vector function

    $$
    \vec{v} = \frac{\hat{r}}{r^2},
    $$

    and compute its divergence. The answer may surprise you... can you explain it?
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    For all $r &gt; 0$, $\nabla\cdot\vec{v} = 0$. The apparent paradox—a field visibly radiating outward from the origin yet having zero divergence—is resolved because the calculation is invalid at $r = 0$, where $\vec{v}$ blows up; in fact, $\nabla\cdot(\hat{r}/r^2) = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec{r})$ (zero everywhere except at the origin, where it is infinite).
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    **Sketch:** $\vec{v} = \hat{r}/r^2$ points radially outward in all directions from the origin, with arrow lengths shrinking rapidly as $1/r^2$ as distance $r$ increases.

    **Divergence for $r \neq 0$:** Write $\vec{v}$ in Cartesian coordinates using $\hat{r} = \vec{r}/r$ and $r = (x^2 + y^2 + z^2)^{1/2}$:

    $$
    \vec{v} = \frac{x\hat{x} + y\hat{y} + z\hat{z}}{r^3} = \frac{x\hat{x} + y\hat{y} + z\hat{z}}{(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}
    $$

    Differentiating the $x$-component using the product rule:

    $$
    \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[x(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)^{-3/2}\right] = (x^2 + y^2 + z^2)^{-3/2} + x\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)^{-5/2}(2x) = \frac{1}{r^3} - \frac{3x^2}{r^5}
    $$

    By symmetry, $\dfrac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} = \dfrac{1}{r^3} - \dfrac{3y^2}{r^5}$ and $\dfrac{\partial v_z}{\partial z} = \dfrac{1}{r^3} - \dfrac{3z^2}{r^5}$. Summing the three derivatives:

    $$
    \nabla\cdot\vec{v} = \frac{3}{r^3} - \frac{3(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)}{r^5} = \frac{3}{r^3} - \frac{3r^2}{r^5} = 0 \qquad (r \neq 0)
    $$

    **Explanation of the paradox:** Visually, the field radiates outward from the origin and looks like a textbook source, so $\nabla\cdot\vec{v} = 0$ seems contradictory. The resolution is that our differentiation assumes $r \neq 0$; at $r = 0$, $\vec{v}$ is singular (blows up). Away from the origin, the spreading of the field lines in solid angle ($\propto r^2$) is exactly compensated by the $1/r^2$ drop in field magnitude, so no *new* flux originates in any shell not containing the origin. All of the "faucet" strength is concentrated at the single point $r = 0$, where $\nabla\cdot(\hat{r}/r^2) = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec{r})$ (as developed in §1.5).
  </div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="1.17" title="Rotational Invariance of the Divergence" tag="Griffiths 1.17 · Scalar Invariance">
  <div slot="problem">
    In two dimensions, show that the divergence transforms as a scalar under rotations.

    *[Hint: Use Eq. 1.29 to determine $\bar{v}_y$ and $\bar{v}_z$, and the method of Prob. 1.14 to calculate the derivatives. Your aim is to show that $\dfrac{\partial \bar{v}_y}{\partial \bar{y}} + \dfrac{\partial \bar{v}_z}{\partial \bar{z}} = \dfrac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \dfrac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}$.]*
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    $\dfrac{\partial \bar{v}_y}{\partial \bar{y}} + \dfrac{\partial \bar{v}_z}{\partial \bar{z}} = \dfrac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \dfrac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}$, confirming that the divergence is invariant (a scalar) under rotations.
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    By Eq. 1.29, the vector components in the rotated frame are:

    $$
    \bar{v}_y = \cos\phi\,v_y + \sin\phi\,v_z, \qquad \bar{v}_z = -\sin\phi\,v_y + \cos\phi\,v_z
    $$

    From Prob. 1.14, the partial derivative operators in the rotated frame are:

    $$
    \frac{\partial}{\partial \bar{y}} = \cos\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \sin\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial \bar{z}} = -\sin\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \cos\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
    $$

    Applying these to $\bar{v}_y$ and $\bar{v}_z$:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\partial \bar{v}_y}{\partial \bar{y}} &= \left(\cos\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \sin\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)\left(\cos\phi\,v_y + \sin\phi\,v_z\right) \\
    &= \cos^2\phi\,\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \sin\phi\cos\phi\,\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial z} + \sin\phi\cos\phi\,\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial y} + \sin^2\phi\,\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\partial \bar{v}_z}{\partial \bar{z}} &= \left(-\sin\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \cos\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)\left(-\sin\phi\,v_y + \cos\phi\,v_z\right) \\
    &= \sin^2\phi\,\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} - \sin\phi\cos\phi\,\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial z} - \sin\phi\cos\phi\,\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial y} + \cos^2\phi\,\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Adding the two expressions cancels all $\sin\phi\cos\phi$ cross terms and leaves:

    $$
    \frac{\partial \bar{v}_y}{\partial \bar{y}} + \frac{\partial \bar{v}_z}{\partial \bar{z}} = (\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi)\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + (\sin^2\phi + \cos^2\phi)\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}
    $$

    Since its value is unchanged by a rotation of axes, the 2D divergence transforms as a scalar.
  </div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="1.19" title="Geometrical Interpretation of Curl via Clockwise Tangent Field" tag="Griffiths 1.19 · Curl Geometry">
  <div slot="problem">
    Draw a circle in the $xy$-plane. At a few representative points draw the vector $\vec{v}$ tangent to the circle, pointing in the clockwise direction. By comparing adjacent vectors, determine the sign of $\partial v_x / \partial y$ and $\partial v_y / \partial x$. According to Eq. 1.41, then, what is the direction of $\nabla\times\vec{v}$? Explain how this example illustrates the geometrical interpretation of the curl.
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    $\dfrac{\partial v_x}{\partial y} &gt; 0$ and $\dfrac{\partial v_y}{\partial x} &lt; 0$, so $\nabla\times\vec{v} = \left(\dfrac{\partial v_y}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}\right)\hat{z}$ points in the **$-\hat{z}$ direction (into the page)**, matching the right-hand rule for a clockwise swirl.
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    Consider a circle centered at the origin in the $xy$-plane with $\vec{v}$ tangent and pointing clockwise (for example, $\vec{v} = \omega(y\hat{x} - x\hat{y})$ with $\omega &gt; 0$):

    - At **9 o'clock** $(-R, 0)$, $\vec{v}$ points straight up ($+y$): $v_x = 0$, $v_y &gt; 0$.
    - Moving clockwise to **10 o'clock** (where both $x$ and $y$ increase), $\vec{v}$ tilts to the right and slightly less upward: $v_x$ increases from $0$ to positive values, while $v_y$ decreases.
    - More generally, moving upward ($+\Delta y$ at fixed $x$) increases the rightward component $v_x$, so $\dfrac{\partial v_x}{\partial y} &gt; 0$; moving rightward ($+\Delta x$ at fixed $y$) decreases the upward component $v_y$ (turning it downward on the right side), so $\dfrac{\partial v_y}{\partial x} &lt; 0$.

    Since $v_z = 0$ and nothing depends on $z$, Eq. 1.41 gives:

    $$
    \nabla\times\vec{v} = \left(\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}\right)\hat{z}
    $$

    Because $\dfrac{\partial v_y}{\partial x} &lt; 0$ and $-\dfrac{\partial v_x}{\partial y} &lt; 0$, the $z$-component is strictly negative: $\nabla\times\vec{v}$ points in the **$-\hat{z}$ direction (into the page)**.

    **Geometrical interpretation:** Curling the fingers of your right hand clockwise (along the flow $\vec{v}$) makes your thumb point *into the page* ($-\hat{z}$). A tiny paddlewheel placed in this flow rotates clockwise, illustrating that $\nabla\times\vec{v}$ measures the local rotation/swirl of $\vec{v}$ with orientation determined by the right-hand rule.
  </div>

<ProblemCard num="1.21" title="Proofs of Product Rules (i), (iv), and (v)" tag="Griffiths 1.21 · Product Rules">
  <div slot="problem">
    Prove product rules (i), (iv), and (v):
    1. $\nabla(fg) = f\nabla g + g\nabla f$.
    2. $\nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) = \vec{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{A}) - \vec{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{B})$.
    3. $\nabla\times(f\vec{A}) = f(\nabla\times\vec{A}) - \vec{A}\times(\nabla f)$.
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    All three identities follow directly by expanding in Cartesian components and applying the ordinary single-variable product rule $\partial_i(uv) = u\,\partial_i v + v\,\partial_i u$.
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    1. **Rule (i) — $\nabla(fg)$:** Expanding in Cartesian components and applying the ordinary product rule to each partial derivative:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \nabla(fg) &= \frac{\partial(fg)}{\partial x}\hat{x} + \frac{\partial(fg)}{\partial y}\hat{y} + \frac{\partial(fg)}{\partial z}\hat{z} \\
    &= \left(f\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} + g\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)\hat{x} + \left(f\frac{\partial g}{\partial y} + g\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)\hat{y} + \left(f\frac{\partial g}{\partial z} + g\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)\hat{z} \\
    &= f\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\hat{x} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}\hat{y} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial z}\hat{z}\right) + g\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\hat{x} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\hat{y} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\hat{z}\right) \\
    &= f\nabla g + g\nabla f
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    2. **Rule (iv) — $\nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})$:** Expanding $\vec{A}\times\vec{B}$ in components:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(A_y B_z - A_z B_y) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(A_z B_x - A_x B_z) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(A_x B_y - A_y B_x) \\
    &= A_y\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} + B_z\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} - A_z\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial x} - B_y\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x} \\
    &\quad + A_z\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y} + B_x\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial y} - A_x\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial y} - B_z\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y} \\
    &\quad + A_x\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial z} + B_y\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z} - A_y\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial z} - B_x\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial z}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Grouping terms proportional to components of $\vec{B}$ and components of $\vec{A}$:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) &= B_x\left(\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial z}\right) + B_y\left(\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x}\right) + B_z\left(\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}\right) \\
    &\quad - A_x\left(\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial z}\right) - A_y\left(\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x}\right) - A_z\left(\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y}\right) \\
    &= \vec{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{A}) - \vec{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{B})
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    3. **Rule (v) — $\nabla\times(f\vec{A})$:** Expanding the curl in Cartesian components:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \nabla\times(f\vec{A}) &= \left[\frac{\partial(fA_z)}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial(fA_y)}{\partial z}\right]\hat{x} + \left[\frac{\partial(fA_x)}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial(fA_z)}{\partial x}\right]\hat{y} + \left[\frac{\partial(fA_y)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial(fA_x)}{\partial y}\right]\hat{z} \\
    &= \left(f\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial y} + A_z\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} - f\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial z} - A_y\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)\hat{x} \\
    &\quad + \left(f\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z} + A_x\frac{\partial f}{\partial z} - f\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x} - A_z\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)\hat{y} \\
    &\quad + \left(f\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} + A_y\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} - f\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y} - A_x\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)\hat{z}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Separating the $f$-multiplied derivatives from the $\partial f$ terms:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \nabla\times(f\vec{A}) &= f\left[\left(\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial z}\right)\hat{x} + \left(\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x}\right)\hat{y} + \left(\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}\right)\hat{z}\right] \\
    &\quad - \left[\left(A_y\frac{\partial f}{\partial z} - A_z\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)\hat{x} + \left(A_z\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} - A_x\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)\hat{y} + \left(A_x\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} - A_y\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)\hat{z}\right] \\
    &= f(\nabla\times\vec{A}) - \vec{A}\times(\nabla f)
    \end{aligned}
    $$
  </div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="1.22" title="The Directional Derivative Operator (A · ∇)B" tag="Griffiths 1.22 · Directional Operator">
  <div slot="problem">
    1. If $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$ are two vector functions, what does the expression $(\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B}$ mean? *(That is, what are its $x$, $y$, and $z$ components, in terms of the Cartesian components of $\vec{A}$, $\vec{B}$, and $\nabla$?)*
    2. Compute $(\hat{r}\cdot\nabla)\hat{r}$, where $\hat{r}$ is the unit vector defined in Eq. 1.21.
    3. For the functions in Prob. 1.15 ($\vec{v}_a = x^2\hat{x} + 3xz^2\hat{y} - 2xz\hat{z}$ and $\vec{v}_b = xy\hat{x} + 2yz\hat{y} + 3zx\hat{z}$), evaluate $(\vec{v}_a\cdot\nabla)\vec{v}_b$.
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    1. $(\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B} = \left(A_x\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x} + A_y\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y} + A_z\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial z}\right)\hat{x} + \left(A_x\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial x} + A_y\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial y} + A_z\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial z}\right)\hat{y} + \left(A_x\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} + A_y\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial y} + A_z\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z}\right)\hat{z}$.
    2. $(\hat{r}\cdot\nabla)\hat{r} = \mathbf{0}$.
    3. $(\vec{v}_a\cdot\nabla)\vec{v}_b = x^2(y + 3z^2)\hat{x} + 2xz(3z^2 - 2y)\hat{y} - 3x^2 z\,\hat{z}$.
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    1. **Meaning of $(\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B}$:** First form the scalar differential operator $\vec{A}\cdot\nabla = A_x\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} + A_y\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} + A_z\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}$, and let it act componentwise on $\vec{B} = B_x\hat{x} + B_y\hat{y} + B_z\hat{z}$:

    $$
    (\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B} = \left(A_x\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x} + A_y\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y} + A_z\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial z}\right)\hat{x} + \left(A_x\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial x} + A_y\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial y} + A_z\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial z}\right)\hat{y} + \left(A_x\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} + A_y\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial y} + A_z\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z}\right)\hat{z}
    $$

    2. **Evaluating $(\hat{r}\cdot\nabla)\hat{r}$:** Write $\hat{r} = \dfrac{\vec{r}}{r} = \dfrac{x\hat{x} + y\hat{y} + z\hat{z}}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}}$. For the $x$-component:

    $$
    \left[(\hat{r}\cdot\nabla)\hat{r}\right]_x = \frac{1}{r}\left(x\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}}\right)
    $$

    Computing the partial derivatives:
    - $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\dfrac{x}{r}\right) = \dfrac{1}{r} - \dfrac{x^2}{r^3}$
    - $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\dfrac{x}{r}\right) = -\dfrac{xy}{r^3}$
    - $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\dfrac{x}{r}\right) = -\dfrac{xz}{r^3}$

    Substituting back gives:

    $$
    \left[(\hat{r}\cdot\nabla)\hat{r}\right]_x = \frac{1}{r}\left[\frac{x}{r} - \frac{x(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)}{r^3}\right] = \frac{1}{r}\left(\frac{x}{r} - \frac{x}{r}\right) = 0
    $$

    By symmetry, the $y$- and $z$-components also vanish, so $(\hat{r}\cdot\nabla)\hat{r} = \mathbf{0}$. *(Geometrically, $\hat{r}$ does not change direction as you move radially outward along $\hat{r}$.)*

    3. **Evaluating $(\vec{v}_a\cdot\nabla)\vec{v}_b$:** Here $\vec{v}_a\cdot\nabla = x^2\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} + 3xz^2\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} - 2xz\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}$ acts on $\vec{v}_b = xy\hat{x} + 2yz\hat{y} + 3zx\hat{z}$:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    (\vec{v}_a\cdot\nabla)\vec{v}_b &= x^2(y\hat{x} + 0\hat{y} + 3z\hat{z}) + 3xz^2(x\hat{x} + 2z\hat{y} + 0\hat{z}) - 2xz(0\hat{x} + 2y\hat{y} + 3x\hat{z}) \\
    &= (x^2 y + 3x^2 z^2)\hat{x} + (6xz^3 - 4xyz)\hat{y} + (3x^2 z - 6x^2 z)\hat{z} \\
    &= x^2(y + 3z^2)\hat{x} + 2xz(3z^2 - 2y)\hat{y} - 3x^2 z\,\hat{z}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

<ProblemCard num="1.23" title="Proofs of Product Rules (ii) and (vi)" tag="Griffiths 1.23 · Vector Product Rules">
  <div slot="problem">
    *(For masochists only.)* Prove product rules (ii) and (vi). Refer to Prob. 1.22 for the definition of $(\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B}$.
    1. $\nabla(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}) = \vec{A}\times(\nabla\times\vec{B}) + \vec{B}\times(\nabla\times\vec{A}) + (\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B} + (\vec{B}\cdot\nabla)\vec{A}$.
    2. $\nabla\times(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) = (\vec{B}\cdot\nabla)\vec{A} - (\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B} + \vec{A}(\nabla\cdot\vec{B}) - \vec{B}(\nabla\cdot\vec{A})$.
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    Both identities are verified by comparing the $x$-component of the LHS and RHS in Cartesian coordinates; the $y$- and $z$-components follow identically by cyclic permutation $(x \to y \to z \to x)$.
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    By cyclic symmetry of $(x,y,z)$, it suffices to prove equality for the $x$-component of each identity.

    1. **Rule (ii) — $x$-component of $\nabla(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B})$:** On the LHS:

    $$
    \left[\nabla(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B})\right]_x = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(A_x B_x + A_y B_y + A_z B_z) = \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x}B_x + A_x\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x}B_y + A_y\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x}B_z + A_z\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x}
    $$

    Now expand the $x$-component of each term on the RHS:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \left[\vec{A}\times(\nabla\times\vec{B})\right]_x &= A_y\left(\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y}\right) - A_z\left(\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x}\right) \\
    \left[\vec{B}\times(\nabla\times\vec{A})\right]_x &= B_y\left(\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}\right) - B_z\left(\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x}\right) \\
    \left[(\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B}\right]_x &= A_x\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x} + A_y\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y} + A_z\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial z} \\
    \left[(\vec{B}\cdot\nabla)\vec{A}\right]_x &= B_x\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x} + B_y\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y} + B_z\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Summing all four RHS pieces, the $y$- and $z$-derivative terms cancel in pairs ($\mp A_y \partial_y B_x$, $\mp A_z \partial_z B_x$, $\mp B_y \partial_y A_x$, $\mp B_z \partial_z A_x$), leaving:

    $$
    B_x\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x} + A_x\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x} + B_y\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} + A_y\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial x} + B_z\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x} + A_z\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} = \left[\nabla(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B})\right]_x
    $$

    2. **Rule (vi) — $x$-component of $\nabla\times(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})$:** On the LHS:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \left[\nabla\times(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})\right]_x &= \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})_z - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})_y = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(A_x B_y - A_y B_x) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(A_z B_x - A_x B_z) \\
    &= \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}B_y + A_x\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial y}B_x - A_y\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}B_x - A_z\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z}B_z + A_x\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    On the RHS, write out the $x$-component:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &\left[(\vec{B}\cdot\nabla)\vec{A} - (\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B} + \vec{A}(\nabla\cdot\vec{B}) - \vec{B}(\nabla\cdot\vec{A})\right]_x \\
    &\quad = \left(B_x\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x} + B_y\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y} + B_z\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z}\right) - \left(A_x\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x} + A_y\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y} + A_z\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial z}\right) \\
    &\qquad + A_x\left(\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z}\right) - B_x\left(\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}\right)
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Canceling $+B_x\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x}$ with $-B_x\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x}$ and $-A_x\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x}$ with $+A_x\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x}$ leaves the exact eight terms of $\left[\nabla\times(\vec{A}\times\vec{B})\right]_x$. Cyclic permutation yields the $y$- and $z$-components.
  </div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="1.24" title="Derivation of the Three Quotient Rules" tag="Griffiths 1.24 · Quotient Rules">
  <div slot="problem">
    Derive the three quotient rules (assuming $g \neq 0$):
    1. $\nabla\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right) = \dfrac{g\nabla f - f\nabla g}{g^2}$.
    2. $\nabla\cdot\left(\dfrac{\vec{A}}{g}\right) = \dfrac{g(\nabla\cdot\vec{A}) - \vec{A}\cdot(\nabla g)}{g^2}$.
    3. $\nabla\times\left(\dfrac{\vec{A}}{g}\right) = \dfrac{g(\nabla\times\vec{A}) + \vec{A}\times(\nabla g)}{g^2}$.
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    All three quotient rules follow directly by expanding in Cartesian coordinates using the single-variable quotient rule (or equivalently by applying product rules (i), (iii), and (v) with scalar factor $1/g$).
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    1. **Gradient quotient rule:**

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \nabla\left(\frac{f}{g}\right) &= \frac{\partial(f/g)}{\partial x}\hat{x} + \frac{\partial(f/g)}{\partial y}\hat{y} + \frac{\partial(f/g)}{\partial z}\hat{z} \\
    &= \frac{g\,\partial_x f - f\,\partial_x g}{g^2}\hat{x} + \frac{g\,\partial_y f - f\,\partial_y g}{g^2}\hat{y} + \frac{g\,\partial_z f - f\,\partial_z g}{g^2}\hat{z} \\
    &= \frac{1}{g^2}\left[g\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\hat{x} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\hat{y} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\hat{z}\right) - f\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\hat{x} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}\hat{y} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial z}\hat{z}\right)\right] \\
    &= \frac{g\nabla f - f\nabla g}{g^2}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    2. **Divergence quotient rule:**

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\vec{A}}{g}\right) &= \frac{\partial(A_x/g)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(A_y/g)}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial(A_z/g)}{\partial z} \\
    &= \frac{g\,\partial_x A_x - A_x\,\partial_x g}{g^2} + \frac{g\,\partial_y A_y - A_y\,\partial_y g}{g^2} + \frac{g\,\partial_z A_z - A_z\,\partial_z g}{g^2} \\
    &= \frac{1}{g^2}\left[g\left(\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}\right) - \left(A_x\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} + A_y\frac{\partial g}{\partial y} + A_z\frac{\partial g}{\partial z}\right)\right] \\
    &= \frac{g(\nabla\cdot\vec{A}) - \vec{A}\cdot(\nabla g)}{g^2}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    3. **Curl quotient rule:** Examining the $x$-component:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \left[\nabla\times\left(\frac{\vec{A}}{g}\right)\right]_x &= \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{A_z}{g}\right) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{A_y}{g}\right) = \frac{g\,\partial_y A_z - A_z\,\partial_y g}{g^2} - \frac{g\,\partial_z A_y - A_y\,\partial_z g}{g^2} \\
    &= \frac{1}{g^2}\left[g\left(\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial z}\right) + \left(A_y\frac{\partial g}{\partial z} - A_z\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}\right)\right] = \frac{g(\nabla\times\vec{A})_x + (\vec{A}\times\nabla g)_x}{g^2}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    The $y$- and $z$-components follow identically by cyclic permutation, yielding $\nabla\times(\vec{A}/g) = \dfrac{g(\nabla\times\vec{A}) + \vec{A}\times(\nabla g)}{g^2}$.
  </div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="1.25" title="Direct Verification of Product Rules (iv), (ii), and (vi)" tag="Griffiths 1.25 · Product Rule Check">
  <div slot="problem">
    1. Check product rule (iv) (by calculating each term separately) for the functions
       $\vec{A} = x\hat{x} + 2y\hat{y} + 3z\hat{z}$ and $\vec{B} = 3y\hat{x} - 2x\hat{y}$.
    2. Do the same for product rule (ii).
    3. Do the same for rule (vi).
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    1. Rule (iv): $\text{LHS} = \nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) = 15z$, and $\text{RHS} = \vec{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{A}) - \vec{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{B}) = 0 - (-15z) = 15z$.
    2. Rule (ii): $\text{LHS} = \nabla(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}) = -y\hat{x} - x\hat{y}$, and $\text{RHS} = (-10y\hat{x} + 5x\hat{y}) + \mathbf{0} + (6y\hat{x} - 2x\hat{y}) + (3y\hat{x} - 4x\hat{y}) = -y\hat{x} - x\hat{y}$.
    3. Rule (vi): $\text{LHS} = \nabla\times(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) = -21y\hat{x} + 10x\hat{y}$, and $\text{RHS} = (3y\hat{x} - 4x\hat{y}) - (6y\hat{x} - 2x\hat{y}) + \mathbf{0} - 6(3y\hat{x} - 2x\hat{y}) = -21y\hat{x} + 10x\hat{y}$.
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    First compute the shared cross product and curls/divergences:

    $$
    \vec{A}\times\vec{B} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{x} & \hat{y} & \hat{z} \\ x & 2y & 3z \\ 3y & -2x & 0 \end{vmatrix} = 6xz\,\hat{x} + 9yz\,\hat{y} - (2x^2 + 6y^2)\hat{z}
    $$

    $$
    \nabla\times\vec{A} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{x} & \hat{y} & \hat{z} \\ \partial_x & \partial_y & \partial_z \\ x & 2y & 3z \end{vmatrix} = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \nabla\times\vec{B} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{x} & \hat{y} & \hat{z} \\ \partial_x & \partial_y & \partial_z \\ 3y & -2x & 0 \end{vmatrix} = (-2 - 3)\hat{z} = -5\hat{z}
    $$

    1. **Product rule (iv): $\nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) = \vec{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{A}) - \vec{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{B})$.**
       - LHS:

       $$
       \nabla\cdot(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(6xz) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(9yz) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(-2x^2 - 6y^2) = 6z + 9z + 0 = 15z
       $$

       - RHS: $\vec{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{A}) = 0$, and $\vec{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{B}) = (3z)(-5) = -15z$, so:

       $$
       \vec{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{A}) - \vec{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{B}) = 0 - (-15z) = 15z \quad \checkmark
       $$

    2. **Product rule (ii): $\nabla(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}) = \vec{A}\times(\nabla\times\vec{B}) + \vec{B}\times(\nabla\times\vec{A}) + (\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B} + (\vec{B}\cdot\nabla)\vec{A}$.**
       - LHS: $\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B} = 3xy - 4xy = -xy$, so:

       $$
       \nabla(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}) = \nabla(-xy) = -y\hat{x} - x\hat{y}
       $$

       - RHS terms:

       $$
       \vec{A}\times(\nabla\times\vec{B}) = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{x} & \hat{y} & \hat{z} \\ x & 2y & 3z \\ 0 & 0 & -5 \end{vmatrix} = -10y\hat{x} + 5x\hat{y}, \qquad \vec{B}\times(\nabla\times\vec{A}) = \mathbf{0}
       $$

       $$
       (\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B} = \left(x\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + 2y\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + 3z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)(3y\hat{x} - 2x\hat{y}) = 6y\hat{x} - 2x\hat{y}
       $$

       $$
       (\vec{B}\cdot\nabla)\vec{A} = \left(3y\frac{\partial}{\partial x} - 2x\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)(x\hat{x} + 2y\hat{y} + 3z\hat{z}) = 3y\hat{x} - 4x\hat{y}
       $$

       - Summing the RHS terms:

       $$
       (-10y\hat{x} + 5x\hat{y}) + \mathbf{0} + (6y\hat{x} - 2x\hat{y}) + (3y\hat{x} - 4x\hat{y}) = -y\hat{x} - x\hat{y} \quad \checkmark
       $$

    3. **Product rule (vi): $\nabla\times(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) = (\vec{B}\cdot\nabla)\vec{A} - (\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B} + \vec{A}(\nabla\cdot\vec{B}) - \vec{B}(\nabla\cdot\vec{A})$.**
       - LHS:

       $$
       \nabla\times(\vec{A}\times\vec{B}) = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{x} & \hat{y} & \hat{z} \\ \partial_x & \partial_y & \partial_z \\ 6xz & 9yz & -2x^2 - 6y^2 \end{vmatrix} = (-12y - 9y)\hat{x} + (6x + 4x)\hat{y} + 0\hat{z} = -21y\hat{x} + 10x\hat{y}
       $$

       - RHS: Here $\nabla\cdot\vec{A} = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6$ and $\nabla\cdot\vec{B} = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0$. Using $(\vec{B}\cdot\nabla)\vec{A}$ and $(\vec{A}\cdot\nabla)\vec{B}$ from part 2:

       $$
       (3y\hat{x} - 4x\hat{y}) - (6y\hat{x} - 2x\hat{y}) + \mathbf{0} - 6(3y\hat{x} - 2x\hat{y}) = (3 - 6 - 18)y\hat{x} + (-4 + 2 + 12)x\hat{y} = -21y\hat{x} + 10x\hat{y} \quad \checkmark
       $$
  </div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="1.26" title="Computing Scalar and Vector Laplacians" tag="Griffiths 1.26 · Laplacian">
  <div slot="problem">
    Calculate the Laplacian of the following functions:
    1. $T_a = x^2 + 2xy + 3z + 4$.
    2. $T_b = \sin x \sin y \sin z$.
    3. $T_c = e^{-5x} \sin 4y \cos 3z$.
    4. $\vec{v} = x^2\hat{x} + 3xz^2\hat{y} - 2xz\hat{z}$.
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    1. $\nabla^2 T_a = 2$
    2. $\nabla^2 T_b = -3\sin x \sin y \sin z$
    3. $\nabla^2 T_c = 0$
    4. $\nabla^2 \vec{v} = 2\hat{x} + 6x\hat{y}$
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    Using the Cartesian Laplacian $\nabla^2 T = \dfrac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 T}{\partial y^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 T}{\partial z^2}$:

    1. For $T_a = x^2 + 2xy + 3z + 4$:

    $$
    \frac{\partial^2 T_a}{\partial x^2} = 2, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 T_a}{\partial y^2} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 T_a}{\partial z^2} = 0 \implies \nabla^2 T_a = 2
    $$

    2. For $T_b = \sin x \sin y \sin z$:

    $$
    \frac{\partial^2 T_b}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\partial^2 T_b}{\partial y^2} = \frac{\partial^2 T_b}{\partial z^2} = -T_b \implies \nabla^2 T_b = -3T_b = -3\sin x \sin y \sin z
    $$

    3. For $T_c = e^{-5x} \sin 4y \cos 3z$:

    $$
    \frac{\partial^2 T_c}{\partial x^2} = 25\,T_c, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 T_c}{\partial y^2} = -16\,T_c, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 T_c}{\partial z^2} = -9\,T_c \implies \nabla^2 T_c = (25 - 16 - 9)T_c = 0
    $$

    4. For the vector $\vec{v} = x^2\hat{x} + 3xz^2\hat{y} - 2xz\hat{z}$, apply $\nabla^2$ to each Cartesian component (Eq. 1.43):
       - $\nabla^2 v_x = \dfrac{\partial^2(x^2)}{\partial x^2} + 0 + 0 = 2$
       - $\nabla^2 v_y = 0 + 0 + \dfrac{\partial^2(3xz^2)}{\partial z^2} = 6x$
       - $\nabla^2 v_z = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0$

    Hence:

    $$
    \nabla^2 \vec{v} = 2\hat{x} + 6x\hat{y}
    $$
  </div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="1.27" title="Vanishing of the Divergence of a Curl" tag="Griffiths 1.27 · Second Derivatives">
  <div slot="problem">
    Prove that the divergence of a curl is always zero. Check it for function $\vec{v}_a$ in Prob. 1.15 ($\vec{v}_a = x^2\hat{x} + 3xz^2\hat{y} - 2xz\hat{z}$).
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{v}) = 0$ identically by the equality of mixed partial derivatives. For $\vec{v}_a$, $\nabla\times\vec{v}_a = -6xz\,\hat{x} + 2z\,\hat{y} + 3z^2\,\hat{z}$, whose divergence is $-6z + 0 + 6z = 0$.
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    **General proof:** Expand $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{v})$ in Cartesian coordinates for any $C^2$ vector field $\vec{v}$:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{v}) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial z}\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial x}\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}\right) \\
    &= \left(\frac{\partial^2 v_z}{\partial x\partial y} - \frac{\partial^2 v_z}{\partial y\partial x}\right) + \left(\frac{\partial^2 v_x}{\partial y\partial z} - \frac{\partial^2 v_x}{\partial z\partial y}\right) + \left(\frac{\partial^2 v_y}{\partial z\partial x} - \frac{\partial^2 v_y}{\partial x\partial z}\right) = 0
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Each parenthesized pair vanishes identically by Clairaut's theorem (equality of mixed second partial derivatives).

    **Check for $\vec{v}_a$:** From Prob. 1.18, $\nabla\times\vec{v}_a = -6xz\,\hat{x} + 2z\,\hat{y} + 3z^2\,\hat{z}$. Taking the divergence:

    $$
    \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{v}_a) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(-6xz) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2z) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(3z^2) = -6z + 0 + 6z = 0 \quad \checkmark
    $$
  </div>
</ProblemCard>

<ProblemCard num="1.28" title="Vanishing of the Curl of a Gradient" tag="Griffiths 1.28 · Second Derivatives">
  <div slot="problem">
    Prove that the curl of a gradient is always zero. Check it for function (b) in Prob. 1.11 ($f(x,y,z) = x^2 y^3 z^4$).
  </div>

  <div slot="answer">
    $\nabla\times(\nabla T) = \mathbf{0}$ identically by the equality of mixed partial derivatives. For $f = x^2 y^3 z^4$, $\nabla\times(\nabla f) = (12x^2 y^2 z^3 - 12x^2 y^2 z^3)\hat{x} + (8xy^3 z^3 - 8xy^3 z^3)\hat{y} + (6xy^2 z^4 - 6xy^2 z^4)\hat{z} = \mathbf{0}$.
  </div>

  <div slot="solution">
    **General proof:** For any $C^2$ scalar function $T(x,y,z)$, compute the curl of $\nabla T$:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \nabla\times(\nabla T) &= \begin{vmatrix} \hat{x} & \hat{y} & \hat{z} \\ \partial/\partial x & \partial/\partial y & \partial/\partial z \\ \partial T/\partial x & \partial T/\partial y & \partial T/\partial z \end{vmatrix} \\
    &= \left(\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial y\partial z} - \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial z\partial y}\right)\hat{x} + \left(\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial z\partial x} - \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x\partial z}\right)\hat{y} + \left(\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x\partial y} - \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial y\partial x}\right)\hat{z} = \mathbf{0}
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    Every component vanishes identically by the equality of mixed partial derivatives.

    **Check for $f = x^2 y^3 z^4$:** From Prob. 1.11(b), $\nabla f = 2xy^3 z^4\,\hat{x} + 3x^2 y^2 z^4\,\hat{y} + 4x^2 y^3 z^3\,\hat{z}$. Taking its curl:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \nabla\times(\nabla f) &= \begin{vmatrix} \hat{x} & \hat{y} & \hat{z} \\ \partial_x & \partial_y & \partial_z \\ 2xy^3 z^4 & 3x^2 y^2 z^4 & 4x^2 y^3 z^3 \end{vmatrix} \\
    &= (12x^2 y^2 z^3 - 12x^2 y^2 z^3)\hat{x} + (8xy^3 z^3 - 8xy^3 z^3)\hat{y} + (6xy^2 z^4 - 6xy^2 z^4)\hat{z} = \mathbf{0} \quad \checkmark
    \end{aligned}
    $$
  </div>
</ProblemCard>
